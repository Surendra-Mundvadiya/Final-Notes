# MODULE 1 — THE IT ACT 2000: AN INTRODUCTION

## 1. Why the Act was made

Before 2000, the law assumed everything was on **paper**. There was no rule saying an electronic document was valid. The Act was passed to:

1. Give a **fillip** (boost) to computers, internet and software in India
2. Provide a **legal framework to promote e-commerce**
3. Bring in an age where **paper would be replaced with electronic records**

**Key dates:** Bill referred to a **42-member Parliamentary Standing Committee** · assent **9 June 2000** · **Act No. 21 of 2000** · commenced **17 October 2000**.

> **Remember:** the original Act was about **e-commerce**, not cyber crime. Cyber crime came in 2008.

## 2. The Legal Basis — UNCITRAL MLEC

- **UNCITRAL** adopted the **Model Law on Electronic Commerce (MLEC)** in **1996**.
- The **UN General Assembly** adopted it by **Resolution A/RES/51/162** on **30 January 1997**.
- The resolution recommends that all States give **favourable consideration** to the Model Law, *"in view of the need for uniformity of the law applicable to alternatives to paper-based methods of communication and storage of information."*

**A Model Law is not binding.** It is only a template.

## 3. The Preamble — three objects

1. Give **legal recognition to transactions** carried out by electronic means — **e-commerce**
2. **Facilitate electronic filing of documents** with Government agencies
3. **Amend four laws:** Indian Penal Code · Indian Evidence Act 1872 · Bankers' Books Evidence Act 1891 · Reserve Bank of India Act 1934

**Why those four?** Electronic activity affected **crime, evidence, banking records and financial regulation**.

## 4. The Three MLEC Principles ★★

```
┌──────────── UNCITRAL MODEL LAW ON E-COMMERCE (1996) ─────────────┐
│                                                                  │
│  1. NON-DISCRIMINATION                    ✔ Act FOLLOWED         │
│     "not invalid ONLY because it is electronic"    ss.3 to 10    │
│                                                                  │
│  2. TECHNOLOGICAL NEUTRALITY              ✘ Act did NOT follow   │
│     s.3 recognised the DIGITAL SIGNATURE only —                  │
│     asymmetric crypto system + hash function                     │
│               │                                                  │
│               └──► CURED by the 2008 Amendment:                  │
│                    s.2(1)(ta) + s.3A + SECOND SCHEDULE           │
│                    (example: Aadhaar biometric authentication)   │
│                                                                  │
│  3. FUNCTIONAL EQUIVALENCE                ✔ Act FOLLOWED         │
│     "writing / signed / original" → what FUNCTION does it serve? │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```


### (i) Non-Discrimination

A document **shall not be denied legal effect, validity or enforceability only because it is in electronic form**.

Note **"only"**. It must still satisfy every other legal requirement.

**Did the original Act follow it? YES** — ss.3 to 10.

### (ii) Technological Neutrality

The law should describe **functions**, not name one technology. Technology changes faster than law, so a neutral rule covers **future developments without a new amendment**.

**Did the original Act follow it? NO. ★ This is the key exam point.**

| | Digital Signature | Electronic Signature |
|---|---|---|
| Defined in | **s.2(1)(p)** | **s.2(1)(ta)** — added 2008 |
| Operative section | **s.3** | **s.3A** — added 2008 |
| Technology | **Fixed** — asymmetric crypto system + hash function | **Open** — any technique "considered reliable" |
| Listed where | In s.3 itself | In the **Second Schedule**, which can be amended |

**s.3(2)** requires an **asymmetric crypto system and hash function** — one technology only. **s.3A** cured this by allowing **any reliable electronic authentication technique specified in the Second Schedule**.

**Example:** **Aadhaar** biometric authentication is not public-key cryptography. It could never be a "digital signature". It works under **s.3A + Second Schedule**.

### (iii) Functional Equivalence

Ask what **function** a paper requirement performs, then let an electronic method perform the same function.

| Paper word | Function | Electronic test |
|---|---|---|
| Writing | Preserves information | Accessible for later reference? |
| Original | Shows integrity | Can integrity be shown? |
| Signed | Shows who agreed | Identifies signer and shows approval? |

**Did the original Act follow it? YES.**

### Summary ★

| Principle | Original IT Act 2000 |
|---|---|
| Non-discrimination | ✔ |
| **Technological neutrality** | **✘ — fixed by 2008 Amendment (s.2(1)(ta), s.3A)** |
| Functional equivalence | ✔ |

## 5. Can the MLEC be used to interpret the Act?

**No** — *Konkan Railway Corporation Ltd. v. Rani Construction (P) Ltd.*, AIR 2002 SC 778:

> *"The Act and the Model Law are **not identically drafted**… the Model Law and judgments and literature thereon are, therefore, **not a guide to the interpretation of the Act**."*

**Meaning:** the Model Law only **influenced** the drafting. Courts must look at the **object of the Act itself**, not the Model Law.

## 6. The IT (Amendment) Act 2008 ★

**Trigger:** the **Mumbai terror attacks (26/11, 2008)**. Notified **5 February 2009**.

| Subject | Provisions |
|---|---|
| **Cyber terrorism** | **s.66F** — up to **life imprisonment** |
| **Electronic signatures** | **s.2(1)(ta)**, **s.3A** |
| **Data protection and privacy** | **s.43A**, **s.72A** |
| **New cyber crimes** | **s.43**, **s.66**, **s.66A**, **ss.66B–66E** |
| **Obscenity / child pornography** | **s.67A**, **s.67B** |
| **Intermediary liability** | **s.2(1)(w)**, **s.79** |
| **Interception, blocking, monitoring** | **s.69A**, **s.69B** |
| **Institutions** | Adjudicating Officer; Cyber Appellate Tribunal |

**The 2008 Amendment changed the Act from an e-commerce law into a law covering crime, security, privacy and platform regulation.**

> **s.66A** was inserted in 2008 but **struck down** in *Shreya Singhal* (2015).

## 8. Section 1 — Scope ★

| Sub-section | Covers |
|---|---|
| **s.1(1)** | Title |
| **s.1(2)** | **Jurisdiction** |
| **s.1(3)** | Commencement — 17 October 2000 |
| **s.1(4)** | **Exclusions — First Schedule** |

### s.1(2) — two types of jurisdiction

**Territorial:** extends to the **whole of India**, **including Jammu & Kashmir**. There is **no exception** for J&K.

**Extra-territorial:** applies to an **offence or contravention committed outside India by any person**.

### Long-arm statute

A law with extra-territorial reach is a **long-arm statute**:

> *"A statute that allows for a state court to obtain personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant on the basis of certain acts committed by an out-of-state defendant, provided that the defendant has a **sufficient connection** with the state."*

### s.1(2) must be read with s.75 ★★

```
            Act committed OUTSIDE India
                        │
                        ▼
        ┌───────────────────────────────┐
        │ Is the person an Indian?      │──► IRRELEVANT   s.75(1)
        └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                        ▼
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ Does the act involve a COMPUTER / COMPUTER   │
   │ SYSTEM / COMPUTER NETWORK LOCATED IN INDIA?  │   s.75(2)
   └──────────┬───────────────────────────┬───────┘
             YES                          NO
              │                            │
              ▼                            ▼
    ★ IT ACT APPLIES                NO JURISDICTION
      (LONG-ARM STATUTE)
```


- **s.75(1):** applies to an offence or contravention committed outside India **irrespective of nationality**
- **s.75(2):** but **only if the act involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India**

**In one line:** s.1(2) gives the reach; **s.75(2) supplies the nexus**. Nationality is irrelevant — the **location of the computer** matters.

**Offence** = punishable as a crime. **Contravention** = attracts compensation (s.43).

### The First Schedule — five entries

| No. | Excluded document |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Negotiable instrument (other than a cheque)** — s.13, NI Act 1881 |
| 2 | **Power-of-attorney** — s.1A, Powers-of-Attorney Act 1882 |
| 3 | **Trust** — s.3, Indian Trusts Act 1882 |
| 4 | **Will** — s.2(h), Indian Succession Act 1925 |
| 5 | **Contract for sale or conveyance of immovable property** |

**Cheques are NOT excluded.**

**Proviso to s.1(4):** the Central Government may **amend the First Schedule by notification**, laid before Parliament.

**Why these were excluded:** in 2000 India was not digitally ready; these documents need **extra formality** (a will needs **two witnesses**); and they carry a **high risk of fraud**. The exclusion is **not permanent**.

## 9. Global Injunctions

```
        Unlawful content is uploaded and goes viral
                            │
          ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
          ▼                                   ▼
   Uploaded from an IP                 Uploaded from an IP
   address INSIDE India                address OUTSIDE India
          │                                   │
          ▼                                   ▼
   ★ GLOBAL TAKEDOWN                   GEO-BLOCK for India only
     (worldwide removal)

              — Swami Ramdev v. Facebook (Delhi HC, 2019)
```


**The problem:** the internet has no borders. If content is blocked only on `.in`, users reach it on `.com`.

### *Swami Ramdev v. Facebook, Inc.* [Delhi HC, 2019] ★

**Facts:** a defamatory video about Swami Ramdev went viral. He sought worldwide removal. The platforms offered only **geo-blocking** for India.

**HELD:**

| Para | Holding |
|---|---|
| **76** | Intermediaries must remove material **"residing in or connected to a computer resource"** — meaning the **network** |
| **78** | Reading **s.2(1)(k)** with **s.79(3)(b)**: where information is uploaded on a computer network, platforms are bound to **remove and disable it from that network completely** |
| **83** | Intermediaries must **"disable access of all kinds"**, including **global viewers** |

**Also:** **mere geo-blocking is not in consonance with s.79**. Since the **IT Act is premised on long-arm jurisdiction**, Indian courts can pass **global injunctions**.

### *Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions* ("Equustek I") [SC Canada, 2017]

**Facts:** Datalink stole Equustek's trade secrets and sold competing products online. Google removed **345 pages from google.ca** but refused to de-index the whole site or touch **google.com**.

**HELD [para 38]:** *"[w]hen a court has **in personam jurisdiction**, and where it is necessary to ensure the injunction's effectiveness, it can grant an injunction enjoining that person's conduct **anywhere in the world**."*

**[para 41]** *"[t]he Internet has no borders – its natural habitat is global."*

**In personam jurisdiction** = power over the **person**, not territory. **De-index** = remove from **search results** only.

### Equustek II and III — in one line each

- **US District Court (N.D. Cal.):** the Canadian order *"undermines the policy goals of **Section 230** and threatens free speech on the global internet"* — so it is **unenforceable in the US**.
- **Supreme Court of British Columbia:** **motion dismissed**. The Canadian court **kept its order**; enforceability abroad is a separate question.

> **The takeaway ★:** there is a difference between **the power to make an order** and **the power to enforce it abroad**.

## 10. Definitions — Section 2(1)

### The three actors ★

```
   ORIGINATOR   ─────────►   INTERMEDIARY   ─────────►   ADDRESSEE
   s.2(1)(za)                 s.2(1)(w)                  s.2(1)(b)

   sends / generates          receives / stores /        intended
   stores / transmits         transmits ON BEHALF        recipient
                              OF ANOTHER PERSON

   ◄───── DYNAMIC ─────►        STATIC          ◄───── DYNAMIC ─────►
   (if the addressee replies, the two roles SWAP; the middle stays put)

   ★ Both the ORIGINATOR and ADDRESSEE definitions EXCLUDE the
     intermediary — this is the doctrinal ROOT of safe harbour.
```


| | **Originator** s.2(1)(za) | **Intermediary** s.2(1)(w) | **Addressee** s.2(1)(b) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Person who **sends, generates, stores or transmits** any electronic message | Person who **on behalf of another person** receives, stores or transmits that message, **or provides any service** with respect to it | Person **intended by the originator** to receive the record |
| **Function** | Sender | **Facilitator** | Recipient |
| **Category** | **Dynamic** | **Static** | **Dynamic** |

**Why dynamic/static:** if I email you and **you reply**, our roles swap — so those two are **dynamic**. The intermediary stays the same — **static**.

**Both definitions exclude the intermediary.** That is the basis of safe harbour.

### Key definitions

| Term | Section | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Computer** | **2(1)(i)** | Electronic, magnetic, optical or **other high-speed data processing device or system** performing **logical, arithmetic and memory functions** by manipulating impulses. **Includes** all connected input, output, processing, storage, software or communication facilities |
| **Communication device** | **2(1)(ha)** | Cell phones, personal digital assistance, or **any other device used to communicate, send or transmit text, video, audio or image** |
| **Computer system** | **2(1)(l)** | A **device or collection of devices**, including input/output support devices, **excluding non-programmable calculators**, capable of being used with **external files** containing programmes and instructions |
| **Computer network** | **2(1)(j)** | **Inter-connection of one or more** computers/computer systems/communication devices through satellite, microwave, wire, wireless etc., **whether or not the inter-connection is continuously maintained** |
| **Computer resource** ★ | **2(1)(k)** | **Computer · communication device · computer system · computer network · data · computer database · software** |
| **Electronic form** | **2(1)(r)** | Information stored in media, magnetic, optical, computer memory, micro film or microfiche |
| **Electronic record** | **2(1)(t)** | **Data, record, image or sound** stored, received or sent in electronic form |
| **Data** | **2(1)(o)** | A **representation** of information/knowledge/facts, prepared in a **formalised manner**, and processed in a computer system or network |
| **Information** | **2(1)(v)** | **Includes** data, message, text, images, sound, voice, codes, computer programmes, software, databases |

**Two points that matter:**

- **"Computer resource" is the widest term in the Act** — it includes hardware **and** software. It was the basis of the global takedown in *Swami Ramdev*.
- **Information is wider; data is information in formalised (structured) form.**

### "Intermediary" — s.2(1)(w) ★

**With respect to any particular electronic record**, any person who **on behalf of another person** receives, stores or transmits that record, **or provides any service** with respect to it.

**Includes:** telecom service providers, network service providers, ISPs, web-hosting service providers, **search engines, online payment sites, online-auction sites, online market places and cyber cafes**.

**Three points:** status is judged **record by record** · the core idea is **acting on behalf of someone else** · the list is **not exhaustive**.

### Are ATMs computers? — *Diebold Systems v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes* [Karnataka HC, 2005]

**HELD [para 39]:** *"…**ATM is not a computer by itself and it is connected to a computer that performs the tasks** requested by the person using ATM's. The computer is connected electronically to many ATM's that may be located from some distance from the computer."*

**Meaning:** the ATM is only the **front-end device**. The **back-end computer** does the processing.

### The chain of intermediaries

```
   USER
    │
    ├─► CYBER CAFÉ ─► ISP ─► DNS PROVIDER ─► WEB HOST
    │                        (name → IP)      (e.g. GoDaddy)
    │
    ├─► SEARCH ENGINE      ├─► VIDEO SHARING   ├─► E-AUCTION
    │   (Google)           │   (YouTube)       │   (eBay)
    │
    ├─► PAYMENT SITE       └─► SOCIAL NETWORK / BLOG
    │   (PayPal)               (Twitter, Blogspot)
    ▼
   PERSON WHO POSTS THE CONTENT / SELLS THE GOODS
   = ORIGINATOR / SELLER — NOT an intermediary
```

> **In a problem, your first job is to say WHICH box you are in.**


There is **no single intermediary** — there is a chain:

**User → Cyber café → ISP → DNS provider → Web host → Search engine → Video sharing site → E-auction site → Payment site → Blogging platform / social network**

At the bottom is the **person who posts the content or sells the goods** — the **originator/seller**, not an intermediary.

> **In a problem, always identify WHICH intermediary in the chain you are dealing with.**

# MODULE 2 — LIABILITY OF INTERMEDIARIES

## 1. Background — why platforms are regulated

**GAMA** = **G**oogle, **A**pple, **M**eta, **A**mazon — the four most valuable companies whose business models rely on online platforms.

**Why the original justification for immunity broke down (from the slide):**

1. Platforms **acquired millions of users** and began **centralising power by subsuming smaller businesses** — Facebook acquired **WhatsApp and Instagram by 2014**
2. **Growing menace of disinformation and misinformation** online
3. **Rampant harassment** of children, women and vulnerable groups
4. Profitability depends on **maximising the time users spend on the platform**
5. So the goal became **"algorithmic recommendation of arresting content that sticks eyeballs to the platform"**

**The conclusion — the most quotable line in this module ★:**

> *"By monitoring users' reading and behaviour the platform companies **ceased to be the neutral conduit** for 'user-generated content' that justified their safe-harbour immunity in the first place."*

## 2. The origin of safe harbour — two US cases

| Case | Facts | Held |
|---|---|---|
| ***Cubby Inc. v. CompuServe Inc.* (1991)** | CompuServe hosted ~150 forums. A defamatory post appeared | **No liability.** **No editorial control.** Compared to a **newsstand** |
| ***Stratton Oakmont v. Prodigy* (1995)** | Prodigy ran a bulletin board "Money Talk". A defamatory post caused a stock to fall | **Liable.** Prodigy **had editorial control policies** |

**The perverse result:** the platform that **moderated** was punished; the one that **did nothing** escaped.

**Section 230, Communications Decency Act 1996 (USA) — the safe-harbour provision:**

> **"No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider."**

**FOSTA** created an **exception**: s.230 does **not apply** to **sex trafficking**, or conduct that **"promotes or facilitates prostitution."**

## 3. Models of Intermediary Liability ★★

| Model | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strict liability** | **Unconditionally liable.** Must monitor in advance. Sanctions include **withdrawal of business licence** and **criminal penalties** | **China, Thailand** |
| **Safe harbour** | **Conditional immunity** — protected only if they comply with requirements laid down by law (notice-and-take-down; filters) | **INDIA** |
| **Broad immunity** | **Expressly excluded from any obligation to monitor.** Treated as **messengers, not publishers** | **USA — s.230 CDA** |

### Two types of safe harbour ★

| | **Vertical** | **Horizontal** |
|---|---|---|
| Liability turns on | The **type of CONTENT** (copyright, defamation, child pornography) | The **FUNCTION performed** by the intermediary |
| Distinguishes hosting from transmitting? | **No** | **Yes** — a mere transmitter may be exempt unconditionally; a host is held to stricter standards |

**India follows the HORIZONTAL model.** That is why s.79(2) is built around **functions** (conduit, caching, hosting), not content categories.

**One universal feature:** **all regimes require intermediaries to remove unlawful content.** Immunity and takedown duties coexist.

## 5. Section 79 — the old version

> *"…no person providing any service as a **network service provider** shall be liable under this Act… for any third party information… **if he proves** that the offence was committed **without his knowledge** or that he had exercised **all due diligence**…"*

**Three points:** narrow term **"network service provider"** · protection only against liability **under this Act** · **burden of proof on the provider**.

## 6. Section 79(1) — the new version ★★

> **"Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force but subject to the provisions of sub-section (2) and (3), an intermediary shall not be liable for any third party information, data, or communication link made available or hosted by him."**

| Phrase | Effect |
|---|---|
| *"Notwithstanding anything contained in any law…"* | **Overriding clause** |
| *"but subject to sub-section (2) and (3)"* | The override is **conditional** |
| *"third party information…"* | Only **someone else's** content is protected |

**So s.79 gives CONDITIONAL immunity, not absolute immunity. Say this in every answer.**

## 7. Section 79(2) — when the exemption applies ★★

### ★★★ THE SAFE HARBOUR DECISION TREE — memorise this shape

```
        Is it an INTERMEDIARY for THIS record?      s.2(1)(w)
                          │ YES
                          ▼
        s.79(2)(a)  conduit / caching / hosting
                         OR
        s.79(2)(b)  PASSIVE — 3 negatives:
                    • does not initiate
                    • does not select the receiver
                    • does not select or modify
                          │ YES
                          ▼
        s.79(2)(c)  DUE DILIGENCE               ★ MANDATORY
                    (Rule 3, IT Rules 2021)       in BOTH cases
                          │ YES
                          ▼
        NOT caught by s.79(3)(a)
        conspired / abetted / aided / induced       (Kedia)
                          │ clear
                          ▼
        NOT caught by s.79(3)(b)
        ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE  *and*  failed to act    (Shreya Singhal)
                          │ clear
                          ▼
              ✔  SAFE HARBOUR AVAILABLE

   ✘ FAIL ANY STEP → s.79(1) does not apply
                   → RULE 7: liable under any law in force
```

**The formula for s.79(2):  [ (a) OR (b) ] + (c) ALWAYS**


### Clause (a) — limited technical function

The intermediary **merely provides access** to third-party information which is transmitted, temporarily stored or hosted:

| | Function |
|---|---|
| **(i)** | Storage for **carrying out transmissions** — **mere conduit** |
| **(ii)** | Storage to make onward transmission **more efficient** — **proxy caching** |
| **(iii)** | Storage of information provided by a subscriber — **hosting** |

### Clause (b) — passive facilitator

The intermediary does **not**:

1. **Initiate the transmission**
2. **Select the receiver** of the transmission, **and**
3. **Select or modify the information** in the transmission

**In plain words:** it did not start it, did not choose who gets it, and did not decide or change what it says.

### Clause (c) — due diligence

The intermediary **observes due diligence** while discharging its duties, **and observes such guidelines as the Central Government may prescribe** (the 2011 Rules, now the **2021 Rules**).

### The interpretation rule ★★

***Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. v. MySpace Inc.* (Delhi HC, 2011):**

> **"The use of the wordings 'or' between (a) and (b) makes them disjunctive, although (c) has to coexist with (a) or (b) whichever is applicable."**

**Formula: [ (a) OR (b) ] + (c) ALWAYS.**

> **Exam trap:** a perfectly passive intermediary **still loses safe harbour** if it fails due diligence. Clause (c) is mandatory in both cases.

## 8. Section 79(3) — when the exemption is LOST ★★

### s.79(3)(a) — active participation

The intermediary has **conspired or abetted or aided or induced**, whether by **threats or promise or otherwise**, in the commission of the unlawful act.

**Case: *Sanjay Kumar Kedia v. Narcotics Control Bureau* (SC, 2007)**

**Facts:** Kedia's companies **designed, developed and hosted pharmaceutical websites** through which **huge quantities of psychotropic substances (Phentermine and Butalbital) were distributed in the USA**.

**HELD:**

> *"…the appellant and his associates were **not innocent intermediaries**… the said business was only a **façade and camouflage for more sinister activity**. In this situation, **Section 79 will not grant immunity**…"*

**Meaning:** merely carrying someone else's information is protected. **Building and running the system as part of the crime** is not.

### s.79(3)(b) — actual knowledge + failure to act

**Two elements, both needed:**

1. **Actual knowledge** that information residing in or connected to a **computer resource controlled by the intermediary** is being used to commit an unlawful act, **AND**
2. **Failure to expeditiously remove or disable access**, **without vitiating the evidence in any manner**

**Knowledge alone is not enough.** There must also be a failure to act.

### Actual knowledge vs general awareness ★★

```
        Someone wants content taken down
                      │
        ┌─────────────┴──────────────┐
        ▼                            ▼
  PRIVATE COMPLAINT            COURT ORDER  or
  (email, tweet, notice)       GOVT / AGENCY NOTIFICATION
        │                            │
        ▼                            ▼
  ✘ NOT actual knowledge      ✔ ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE
    (Shreya Singhal,            + must relate to an
     para 119(c))                 Art.19(2) ground
        │                            │
        ▼                            ▼
  NO duty to remove          Remove within 3 HOURS
  Safe harbour intact        (Rule 3(1)(d), as amended 2026)
                                     │
                                     ▼
                             PRESERVE for 180 DAYS
                             (Rule 3(1)(g))
```


| | **General awareness** | **Actual knowledge** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A vague sense that illegal content exists somewhere on the platform | **Specific knowledge of the particular unlawful material** |
| Defeats safe harbour? | **NO** | **YES** |

## 9. *Shreya Singhal* — the s.79 holding ★★

**HELD [para 119(c)]:**

> **"Section 79 is valid subject to Section 79(3)(b) being read down to mean that an intermediary upon receiving actual knowledge from a court order or on being notified by the appropriate government or its agency that unlawful acts relatable to Article 19(2) are going to be committed then fails to expeditiously remove or disable access to such material. Similarly, the Information Technology 'Intermediary Guidelines' Rules, 2011 are valid subject to Rule 3 sub-rule (4) being read down in the same manner."**

**"Reading down"** = giving a provision a **narrower meaning** so it stays constitutional, instead of striking it down.

**Three points ★:**

1. **s.79 is VALID.**
2. **s.79(3)(b) is READ DOWN** — "actual knowledge" means **(a) a court order**, or **(b) notification by the appropriate Government or its agency**, and the unlawful acts must be **relatable to Article 19(2)**.
3. **Rule 3(4) of the 2011 Rules is read down the same way.**

**Why:** before this, a **private complaint** could force a takedown. That would make the platform remove first and think later, and turn it into a **judge of legality**. The Court put that decision back with **courts and authorised Government agencies**.

## 10. Corporate and criminal liability — the Bazee.com cases

### *Avnish Bajaj v. State* (Bazee.com)

**Provisions:** **s.292 IPC** (obscenity in print media) · **s.294 IPC** (obscene acts in public places) · **s.67 IT Act** (obscene content in electronic form) read with **s.85** (offences by companies).

**The legal question:**

> **Whether a director could be held criminally liable for offences attributable to a company, when the company itself is not arraigned as an accused?**

**Delhi High Court:** **discharged** him under **ss.292 and 294 IPC** (the IPC does not recognise automatic director liability), **but held** a case could be made under **s.67 read with s.85 IT Act**. **s.79 did not figure in the arguments at all.**

**Supreme Court (2012) — overturned:**

> **Vicarious liability cannot be fastened on Avnish Bajaj because the company was not arraigned as an accused. "Commission of an offence by the company was an express CONDITION PRECEDENT to attract the liability of others in charge of the company."**

**Meaning:** prosecute the **company first**. Only then can you proceed against the director.

### *Sharat Babu Digumarti v. Govt of NCT of Delhi* (2016)

The trial court dropped **s.294 IPC** and **s.67 IT Act** but **framed a charge under s.292 IPC**. The Delhi HC upheld it. **The Supreme Court quashed the proceedings.**

**HELD:**

> **Electronic forms of transmission are covered by the IT Act, which is a SPECIAL LAW. If the offender has been acquitted under the relevant provisions of the IT Act, he cannot be proceeded against under the IPC for the same offence.**

**Meaning:** for anything in **electronic form**, the **IT Act applies, not the IPC**. Special law prevails over general law.

## 11. "Actual knowledge" — the IP cases ★★

### *MySpace* — Single Bench (Delhi HC)

**HELD:**

1. **Infringement under s.51(a)(ii) Copyright Act had occurred** — **web space is a "place"**, and there were **monetary gains in the form of ad revenue**
2. **MySpace modifies the format of the content**, so the **"no knowledge" defence was rejected**
3. **s.79 would be overridden by s.81's non-obstante clause**

**Section 81 — remember the proviso:**

> The provisions of this Act shall have effect **notwithstanding anything inconsistent** in any other law.
> **Provided that** nothing in this Act shall restrict any person from exercising any right under the **Copyright Act 1957** or the **Patents Act 1970**.

### *MySpace* — Division Bench (Delhi HC, 2016) ★★ **leading case**

**Three issues:** (i) did MySpace have **knowledge**? (ii) does **s.81 override the safe harbour** in copyright cases? (iii) should **ss.79 and 81 IT Act and s.51 Copyright Act be read harmoniously**?

**HELD:**

1. **To attract liability for secondary infringement, MySpace should have had ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE and NOT MERE AWARENESS.**
2. **The proviso does not override the safe harbour.** It **cannot be denied** in a copyright action. **The three sections must be read harmoniously.**
3. The Court directed **SCIL to provide a specific catalogue of infringing works which also pointed to the URL of the files**. On receiving such specific knowledge, MySpace must **remove the content within 36 HOURS**.

**Para 57:**

> *"**If copyright owners… inform MySpace specifically about infringing works and despite such notice it does not takedown the content, then alone is safe harbor denied.** However, **it is for SCIL to show** that despite giving specific information the appellant did not comply with its notice."*

**Two rules from para 57:** specific notice **first**, failure **second**, loss of safe harbour **only then** · and **the burden is on the rightsholder**.

### *Kent RO Systems v. Amit Kotak* (Delhi HC, 2017)

**HELD:**

1. **Refused to compel intermediaries to screen IP-infringing content on an *ex ante* basis** (before any complaint)
2. Compelling screening would be **"an unreasonable interference with the rights of the intermediary to carry on its business"**
3. It would change the intermediary's role **from a FACILITATOR to an ADJUDICATOR**
4. But **once specific infringing products are brought to its attention, it is liable to remove those listings**

**The rule:** **no duty to check everything in advance; a duty to act on a specific complaint.**

### *Christian Louboutin SAS v. Nakul Bajaj* (Delhi HC, 2018) ★★

**Facts:** **Darveys.com** sold "Christian Louboutin" products, used the founder's **name, images and meta-tags**, charged a **membership fee**, and was **not an authorised seller**. It claimed protection as an **intermediary**.

**HELD — Darveys.com cannot be termed an intermediary entitled to protection under s.79.**

**Para 78:**

> *"When an e-commerce company claims exemption under Section 79 of the IT Act, **it ought to ensure that it does not have an active participation in the selling process. The presence of any elements which shows active participation could deprive intermediaries of the exemption.**"*

**The 26 activities ★★** — the judgment listed **26 activities** distinguishing an **active** from a **passive** intermediary. Examples:

1. **Identification of the seller** and providing seller details
2. **Providing transport** for the seller to send the product to the warehouse
3. **Uploading the entry** of the product
4. **Providing authenticity guarantees**
5. **Creation of the listing**
6. **Providing or uploading reviews**
7. **Advertising the product**
8. **Packing the product** in the platform's own branded box
9. **Transporting the product** to the purchaser
10. **Accepting cash** for the sale

```
  PASSIVE INTERMEDIARY   ◄───────────────►   ACTIVE PARTICIPANT
  ✔ keeps s.79                                ✘ LOSES s.79

  Tick how many of the 26 activities the platform performs:

   □ identifies the seller        □ packs in its own branded box
   □ provides transport           □ transports to the purchaser
   □ uploads the product entry    □ accepts cash for the sale
   □ creates the listing          □ uses the mark in meta-tags
   □ guarantees authenticity      □ advertises the product
   □ uploads or provides reviews  □ enrols and contracts sellers

        MORE ticks  ──────────►  closer to ACTIVE PARTICIPANT

        — Christian Louboutin v. Nakul Bajaj (Delhi HC 2018), para 78
```

**In the exam:** know there are **26**, name **six to eight**, and apply the test — **the more of these a platform does, the more it is an active participant, and the more likely it loses s.79.**

### *Neetu Singh v. Telegram* (Delhi HC, 2022) ★★

*(Full case study in Part 5.)*

**Facts:** video lectures of Ms. Neetu Singh were **uploaded daily on Telegram channels** and sold to students **at discounted rates**. The plaintiffs sought **takedown** and **disclosure of the identity of the channel operators**.

**Telegram's defences:** **right to privacy** (*Puttaswamy*) · **IT Rules 2021** · **servers in Singapore** with encrypted data, governed by **Singapore's PDPA 2012**, so **decryption not possible**.

**HELD:**

1. The lectures are **"cinematograph films" (s.2(f))** and the course material **"literary works" (s.2(o))** under the Copyright Act. The copies on Telegram are **infringing copies**.
2. **On jurisdiction:** *"Courts in India would be perfectly justified in directing Telegram, which runs its massive operations in India, to adhere to Indian law… **Infringers cannot be permitted to seek shelter under Telegram's policies merely on the ground that its physical server is in Singapore.**"*
3. **On privacy:** *"The right to freedom of speech or the right to life including the right to privacy **cannot be used by any person or entity, let alone an infringer, in order to escape the consequences of illegal actions.**"*

**The three-part test for disclosure (from *Puttaswamy*) ★:**

1. **A law must exist** authorising disclosure
2. **Disclosure must be needed**, given the nature of the encroachment
3. **It must not be disproportionate**

**Direction:** Telegram to disclose **mobile numbers, IP addresses, email addresses etc. of the channels/devices — to the Court in a SEALED COVER.**

## 12. Expanding obligations — four short cases

### *Google v. Visakha Industries*

**Facts:** criminal defamation over posts on a Google group about asbestos. Google argued it was **neither publisher nor endorser**, only a platform. The **Andhra Pradesh High Court refused**, because **Google failed to remove the defamatory material**.

**HELD:**

> *"there may be **publication within the meaning of Section 499 of the IPC** even in the case of an internet operator, **if having the power and the right and the ability to remove a matter, upon being called upon to do so, there is a refusal to do so.**"*

**Three conditions:** the operator has the **power, right and ability** to remove → it is **called upon** → it **refuses**.

### *Registrar (Judicial), Madurai Bench* (2018) — Blue Whale Challenge

The Madras HC took ***suo motu* cognizance**. **HELD:** online services **"cannot abdicate their duties and responsibilities under law"**. The Court **directed the Central Government to bring "Over The Top" (OTT) services into a legal framework** obliging them to **comply with Indian laws** and **provide information to law enforcement**.

### *Sabu Mathew George v. UOI* (2018)

**Facts:** a writ petition to **ban pre-natal sex determination advertisements** from search engines, as violating **s.22 of the PCPNDT Act 1994**.

**DIRECTED:**

1. Google, Microsoft and Yahoo to **"AUTO-BLOCK"** such advertisements
2. Each search engine to form an **expert in-house committee** to evaluate and delete violating content
3. The Central Government to **constitute a nodal agency**
4. The nodal agency to convey **actionable complaints**, which intermediaries must **delete within 36 HOURS** and intimate the agency

> **Note the tension:** this case **required proactive filtering**, which *Kent RO* and *MySpace* refused. It is special because it enforces a **statutory advertising ban**.

### *Kamlesh Vaswani v. UOI* (2013)

A PIL challenging **ss.66, 67, 69, 71, 72, 75, 79, 80 and 85** as **unconstitutional** for being **inefficient against pornography**, and seeking that **watching pornographic videos be made a non-bailable, cognizable offence**.

**Outcome:** **857 pornography websites were blocked in August 2015, but all were unblocked within a few days** — keyword filters are blunt and risk catching legitimate medical and scientific content.

## 13. *X v. Union of India* (Karnataka HC, 2025) ★★

**Challenged:** the Government's power to issue takedown orders under **s.79(3)(b)**; the constitutionality of **Rule 3(1)(d)**; and the **"Sahyog Portal"** as ***ultra vires***.

### Section 69A vs Section 79(3)(b) ★★

```
 ┌──────────────────────────────┐  ┌──────────────────────────────┐
 │        SECTION 69A           │  │      SECTION 79(3)(b)        │
 ├──────────────────────────────┤  ├──────────────────────────────┤
 │ A GOVERNMENT POWER           │  │ A CONDITION ON IMMUNITY      │
 │ to BLOCK information         │  │ — confers NO blocking power  │
 │                              │  │                              │
 │ Grounds: sovereignty,        │  │ Ground: violation of         │
 │ security, public order, etc. │  │ "any law for the time        │
 │                              │  │  being in force"             │
 │ BLOCKING RULES 2009:         │  │                              │
 │   • opportunity of hearing   │  │ NO separate procedure        │
 │   • written reasons          │  │                              │
 │   • review committee         │  │ Do not comply →              │
 │                              │  │ FORFEIT s.79 immunity        │
 └──────────────────────────────┘  └──────────────────────────────┘

        X v. Union of India (Karnataka HC, 2025):
        the SAHYOG PORTAL is within the four corners of the law.
```


| | **Section 69A** | **Section 79(3)(b)** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **A Government POWER to block information** | **A CONDITION on the intermediary's immunity** |
| Grounds | **Sovereignty, security, public order** etc. | Violation of **"any law for the time being in force"** |
| Procedure | **Detailed process under the Blocking Rules 2009** — **hearing** and **written reasons** | **No separate procedure** |
| Confers blocking power? | **YES** | **NO** — but intermediaries must comply with lawful notifications **or forfeit immunity** |

**The argument in one line:** s.69A has safeguards; s.79(3)(b) does not. So the Government should not use s.79(3)(b) as a substitute for s.69A.

**HELD:** the **Sahyog Portal is within the four corners of the law**. Under **s.79(3)(b)**, if a lawful notification is sent, **the intermediary must take the content down, or it forfeits immunity**.

## 14. *Telegram v. Union of India* (Delhi HC, 2026) ★

**Background:** a **NEET examination fraud** matter. A **s.69A** blocking order was made against the **whole Telegram platform**.

**Telegram's arguments:**

| Argument | Substance |
|---|---|
| **Compliance** | It had **removed 900 of 1300 flagged URLs** |
| **Moderation** | It used **AI, human moderators, and prompt enforcement** |
| **Disproportionality** | A platform-wide ban was **not the least restrictive method** and affected **150 million lawful users** |
| ***Ultra vires*** | s.69A allows blocking of specific **"information"**, **not whole platforms** |

**HELD:** the Government had **recorded sufficient reasons**, **especially in an emergency context**; the process **conformed with s.69A and the Blocking Rules** (interim order, opportunity to be heard, review); and **proportionality was satisfied**.

### The proportionality test ★★

```
   STEP 1   Is there a LEGITIMATE AIM?            ──► NO → FAILS
              │ YES
   STEP 2   Is the measure BACKED BY LAW?         ──► NO → FAILS
              │ YES
   STEP 3   Is there a RATIONAL NEXUS between     ──► NO → FAILS
            the measure and the object?
              │ YES
   STEP 4   Is it the LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS?      ──► NO → FAILS
              │ YES
              ▼
        ★ THE MEASURE IS PROPORTIONATE

   Authorities: K.S. Puttaswamy · Anuradha Bhasin
   Step 4 is where most measures fail — attack it first.
```


| Step | Test |
|---|---|
| **1** | There must be a **legitimate aim** |
| **2** | The measure must be **backed by law** |
| **3** | There must be a **rational nexus** between measure and object |
| **4** | It must be the **least intrusive** means available |

**Authorities: *K.S. Puttaswamy* and *Anuradha Bhasin*.**

> **Use this test whenever a problem involves blocking, shutdown, takedown or disclosure.**

### Rule 7 — the sanction ★★

> **If an intermediary fails to observe these Rules, s.79(1) SHALL NOT APPLY, and the intermediary shall be liable for punishment under any law in force.**

**This is the enforcement engine.** End every intermediary problem answer with Rule 7.

### Key rules to know

| Rule | Duty |
|---|---|
| **3(1)(d)** | On **actual knowledge** (court order or Government notification), **remove or disable access** — **36 hours**, now **3 HOURS** under the 2026 Rules |
| **3(1)(g)** | **Preserve** removed information and records for **180 DAYS** for investigation |
| **3(1)(j)** | Provide **information or assistance** to a lawfully authorised Government agency within **72 HOURS** |
| **3(2)** | Publish the **Grievance Officer's name and contact**; acknowledge a complaint in **24 hours**; dispose of it in **15 days**, now **7 DAYS** |

### Rule 4 — additional duties of an SSMI ★★

| Rule | Duty |
|---|---|
| **4(1)(a)** | **Chief Compliance Officer** — responsible for ensuring compliance; **liable** if due diligence is not observed; must be **resident in India** |
| **4(1)(b)** | **Nodal Contact Person** — **24×7 coordination with law enforcement agencies**; **resident in India** |
| **4(1)(c)** | **Resident Grievance Officer** — performs the Rule 3(2) functions; **resident in India** |
| **4(2)** | **Identification of the FIRST ORIGINATOR** of information — for messaging services |
| **4(4)** | **Endeavour to deploy automated tools** to proactively identify **rape and child sexual abuse material**, and content **identical** to what was previously removed |
| **4(7)** | Enable users to **voluntarily verify** their accounts and receive a **visible mark of verification** |
| **4(8)** | Where the SSMI removes content **on its own accord**, it must give the user **prior notification with reasons**, an **opportunity to dispute**, and **RGO oversight** |

### Rule 4(2) — the first originator, with its provisos ★★

**Triggered only by:** a **judicial order of a competent court**, **or** an order under **s.69** through the **2009 Interception Rules**.

| Proviso | Effect |
|---|---|
| **1** | Only for offences relating to **sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order**, incitement to those, or **rape, sexually explicit material or child sexual abuse material**, punishable with **imprisonment of not less than FIVE YEARS** |
| **2** | **No order** where **less intrusive means** are effective in identifying the originator |
| **3** | The intermediary **shall not be required to disclose the CONTENTS** of any message |
| **4** | Where the first originator is **outside India**, the **first originator within India is deemed to be the first originator** |

**Why it exists:** a false message goes viral and causes riots or mob lynching, but nobody knows who started it.

**Against it:** traceability requires **weakening encryption**; the deeming provision may catch an **innocent Indian forwarder**; and it has a **chilling effect**.

## 16. Part III — Code of Ethics for Digital Media

**The gap it filled:** **print media** had the Press Council; **TV** had the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act 1995; **digital media and OTT had nothing** until 2021.

### Rule 8 — who is covered (the "definitional jigsaw") ★

| Term | Rule | Covered? |
|---|---|---|
| **News and current affairs content** — made available **over the internet** | **2(1)(m)** | **YES** |
| **Newspaper** — the same content on **loosely folded sheets with newsprint** | **2(1)(n)** | **NO** |
| **Publisher of news and current affairs content** | **2(1)(t)** | **YES** |

**The same news is regulated differently depending on the MEDIUM.** This asymmetry was a ground of challenge under **Article 14** in *Kunal Kamra*.

### The Code of Ethics — Part B (OTT)

A publisher **shall exercise due caution and discretion** in relation to content which:

- **affects the sovereignty and integrity of India**
- **jeopardises the security of the State**
- is **detrimental to India's friendly relations with foreign countries**
- is **likely to incite violence or disturb public order**

And must consider **India's multi-racial and multi-religious context**.

### Content classification — the five ratings ★

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **U** | All ages |
| **U/A 7+** | 7 years and above, with parental guidance |
| **U/A 13+** | 13 years and above |
| **U/A 16+** | 16 years and above |
| **A** | **Adults only** |

**Plus: access control (parental locks) for U/A 13+ and above, and reliable age verification for "A".**

**Contrast with film:** CBFC certifies films externally and can order cuts. **OTT publishers SELF-classify.** That is the "light touch" model.

# MODULE 3 — CYBER-SPEECH

## 1. Why online speech is a separate problem

| | **Web 1.0** | **Web 2.0** |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Publishing** | **Participation** |
| The user is a | **Consumer** | **Prosumer** (Producer + Consumer) |

Web 1.0 was **read-only** — a few publishers, everyone else read. Web 2.0 made **every user a publisher**. Millions now speak instantly through privately owned platforms.

**"Facebookistan"** (Anupam Chander): *"Who rules Facebookistan?… **regulatory power is, de facto, dispersed across a wide array of international actors.**"* A platform is so large it behaves almost like a country, and **nobody fully controls it**.

## 2. The two constitutional models ★★

### USA — First Amendment

> *"**CONGRESS SHALL MAKE NO LAW**… abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press…"*

The wording is **absolute** — **no grounds of restriction appear in the text**. The US treats speech as a **marketplace of ideas**; broadly only **obscenity** and **IP violations** are unprotected.

### Europe — ECHR Article 10

**Two threshold conditions before any restriction is valid:**

1. It must be **prescribed by law**
2. It must be **necessary in a democratic society**

**Then it must fit one of eight listed interests:** national security · territorial integrity · public safety · prevention of disorder or crime · protection of health or morals · protection of the reputation or rights of others · preventing disclosure of information received in confidence · maintaining the authority and impartiality of the judiciary.

Article 10 also says expression **"carries with it duties and responsibilities"**, and protects it **"regardless of frontiers"**.

### India — Article 19(1)(a) and 19(2)

```
  ARTICLE 19(1)(a) — freedom of speech and expression
        │  includes speech over the INTERNET (Anuradha Bhasin)
        ▼
  ARTICLE 19(2) — reasonable restrictions on EIGHT grounds,
                  and ONLY these eight:

    1  Sovereignty and integrity of India
    2  Security of the State
    3  Friendly relations with foreign States
    4  Public order
    5  Decency or morality
    6  Contempt of court
    7  Defamation
    8  Incitement to an offence

  ✘ "FALSITY" IS NOT ONE OF THEM  ── Kunal Kamra (Bom HC 2024)
```


**India follows the European model, not the American one** — a right plus a list of permitted restrictions.

**The eight grounds of Article 19(2) ★:** **sovereignty and integrity of India · security of the State · friendly relations with foreign States · public order · decency or morality · contempt of court · defamation · incitement to an offence.**

> **Memorise these eight.** Every question in this module turns on them.

## 3. *LICRA v. Yahoo!* (2000)

**Facts:** **Yahoo! Inc. (USA)** hosted an auction site selling **Nazi memorabilia**; **Yahoo France** provided links and access. In France this is **illegal**.

**HELD (French Tribunal):** access by French users was an **offence under French law**. Ordered **Yahoo! Inc.** to *"take such measures as may be necessary to prevent the exhibition or sale on its Yahoo.com site of Nazi objects **throughout the territory of France**"*, and **Yahoo France** to warn users.

**Yahoo's two defences, both rejected:**

1. The **Paris court was not competent** (no jurisdiction) — **rejected**
2. Compliance was **technologically impossible** — **rejected on the basis of expert witnesses' reports**

**Enforcement:** a **3-month deadline** and a penalty of **100,000 francs PER DAY**. **Yahoo eventually complied.**

**In the USA:** Yahoo sued LICRA and UEJF in the **US District Court (N.D. California)**, saying the French order breached the **First Amendment**. The **District Court upheld** Yahoo's claim, but the **Court of Appeals reversed in 2004 — on the ground that the District Court lacked jurisdiction over UEJF and LICRA.**

> **The lesson:** the **same content can be a crime in one country and protected speech in another**.

## 4. Hate speech — the framework

**UN definition:** communication that **attacks or discriminates against individuals or groups based on identity factors like religion, ethnicity, or gender**. But **there is no universal legal definition of hate speech under international human rights law**.

**Three attributes ★:**

1. **Form** — any form of expression, including **images, cartoons, memes, objects, gestures and symbols**, **offline or online**
2. **Character** — **"discriminatory"** (biased, bigoted, intolerant) or **"pejorative"** (prejudiced, contemptuous, demeaning)
3. **Target** — **real or perceived "identity factors"**: religion, ethnicity, nationality, race, colour, descent, gender, and also language, social origin, disability, health status, sexual orientation

**18 June** is the **International Day for Countering Hate Speech**.

### *Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. Union of India* (AIR 2014 SC 1591)

**Facts:** a **PIL** seeking directions to stop **inflammatory speeches by politicians and religious leaders**.

**HELD:** a bench headed by **Justice B.S. Chauhan REFUSED to frame guidelines** and **asked the Law Commission** to give recommendations to the Centre.

**The legal framework the Court surveyed ★ — India has NO single hate speech law. It is spread across nine statutes:**

| Statute | Provisions |
|---|---|
| **Indian Penal Code, 1860** | ss. **124A, 153A, 153B, 295-A, 298, 505(1), 505(2)** |
| **Representation of People Act, 1951** | ss. **8, 123(3A), 125** |
| **IT Act, 2000 & Intermediaries Guidelines Rules, 2011** | ss. **66A, 69, 69A**; Rules **3(2)(b), 3(2)(i)** |
| **Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973** | ss. **95, 107, 144, 151, 160** |
| **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967** | ss. **2(f), 10, 11, 12** |
| **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** | s. **7** |
| **Religious Institutions (Prevention of Misuse) Act, 1980** | ss. **3, 6** |
| **Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995** | ss. **5, 6, 11, 12, 16, 17, 19, 20** |
| **Cinematograph Act, 1952** | ss. **4, 5B, 7** |

### *Campaign Against Hate Speech v. State of Karnataka* (Karnataka HC, 2020)

**Facts:** action sought against **media houses** for hate speech in reporting that linked the **Covid-19 outbreak** to specific communities.

**HELD — the Court declined:**

1. It would be **improper to define "hate speech"** because of the absence of **"any specific legislation and precise legislative definition given by Parliament"**
2. It was **for Parliament** to decide reasonable restrictions under **Article 19(2)**
3. Since Parliament had **not legislated on or defined hate speech**, the Court **could not issue directions under Article 226**

## 5. Section 66A and *Shreya Singhal* ★★★

### What s.66A covered

| Limb | Message | Conduct it reached |
|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | **Grossly offensive or menacing** | Obscenity, **morphing of images**, defamation, **cyber-bullying, stalking** |
| **(b)** | **Known to be false**, causing **annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill will** | Criminal intimidation, extortion, **public mischief**, **mob lynching via fake news** |
| **(c)** | Causing **annoyance or inconvenience**, or **misleading about the origin** of the message | **Spamming, unsolicited emails/calls** |

**s.66A was the only provision reaching cyber-bullying, trolling, spam, stalking, morphing and fake news.**

### *Shreya Singhal v. UoI* (AIR 2015 SC 1523) — the four grounds ★★★

**Ground 1 — No proximate relationship** between the restriction and the act.

**Ground 2 — The three forms of speech (THE CORE TEST):**

```
   PROTECTED  ◄──────────────────────────────►  RESTRICTABLE

   DISCUSSION   ───►   ADVOCACY   ───►   │   INCITEMENT
   exchanging          actively          │   urging or provoking
   ideas               supporting        │   unlawful action
                       a cause           │
                                         │
   ───── Art.19(1)(a) PROTECTS ──────────┤── Art.19(2) MAY restrict

   "even if OFFENSIVE or UNPOPULAR"          ★ only past this line

                            — Shreya Singhal v. UoI (2015)
```


| Form | Meaning | Protected? |
|---|---|---|
| **Discussion** | Exchanging ideas | **Protected** |
| **Advocacy** | Actively supporting a cause | **Protected** |
| **Incitement** | Urging unlawful action | **May be restricted** |

> **"a speech can only be limited on grounds of the exceptions mentioned in Article 19(2) when it reaches the THRESHOLD OF INCITEMENT. All other forms of speech, even if OFFENSIVE or UNPOPULAR, have to be protected under Article 19(1)(a)."**

**Learn this sentence word for word.** It wins marks in almost every speech problem.

**Ground 3 — Vagueness:**

> **"What may be offensive to one may not be offensive to another… Even the expression 'persistently' is completely imprecise — suppose a message is sent thrice, can it be said that it was sent 'persistently'? Does a message have to be sent (say) at least eight times…? There is no demarcating line conveyed by any of these expressions — and that is what renders the Section unconstitutionally vague."**

**Ground 4 — Chilling effect:** people stop speaking lawfully for fear of arrest or prosecution.

### The full result of *Shreya Singhal* ★★

```
  ┌──────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ s.66A            │ ✘ STRUCK DOWN in its entirety        │
  ├──────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ s.69A + Blocking │ ✔ UPHELD                             │
  │ Rules 2009       │                                      │
  ├──────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ s.79(3)(b)       │ ⚠ READ DOWN — actual knowledge means │
  │                  │   a COURT ORDER or a GOVT/AGENCY     │
  │                  │   NOTIFICATION, on an Art.19(2) ground│
  ├──────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Rule 3(4),       │ ⚠ READ DOWN in the same manner       │
  │ 2011 Rules       │                                      │
  └──────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────┘

  THE FOUR GROUNDS for striking down s.66A:
    1  No PROXIMATE RELATIONSHIP with any Art.19(2) ground
    2  No distinction between DISCUSSION / ADVOCACY / INCITEMENT
    3  VAGUENESS  ("offensive" · "annoyance" · "persistently")
    4  CHILLING EFFECT
```


| Provision | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **s.66A** | **STRUCK DOWN** entirely |
| **s.69A + Blocking Rules 2009** | **UPHELD** |
| **s.79(3)(b)** | **READ DOWN** — actual knowledge = **court order or Government notification** |
| **Rule 3(4), 2011 Rules** | **READ DOWN** the same way |

### After *Shreya Singhal* — the "vacuum" debate

**"Throwing the baby away with the bathwater":** the Court struck down the **whole** provision instead of separating the good from the bad. The **"baby"** is the need to address digital harms; the **"bathwater"** is the vague drafting.

**Harms left behind:** *"In India **cyber bullying and trolling, online gender harassment, smishing and vishing** are becoming rampant."*

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Trolling** | Deliberately provocative or abusive online behaviour |
| **Smishing** | **SMS + phishing** — deceptive text messages |
| **Vishing** | **Voice + phishing** — deception by phone call |

**An expert committee** recommended amending the **IPC, CrPC and IT Act** to introduce **stringent provisions specifying punishment** for hate speech and online incitement. The **Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home** said the removal of s.66A had **left a VOID**. But **consultations failed and no replacement was ever enacted.**

## 6. Recent controversies — short notes

**Justice Shekhar Kumar Yadav:** a **sitting Allahabad HC judge** spoke at a **Vishwa Hindu Parishad** event, appearing to endorse **majoritarian rule** using phrases like *'humaari Gita, aapki Quran'*. On **17 December 2024** he appeared before the **Supreme Court Collegium**, which said the remarks were **"avoidable"**. On **1 February 2025**, after his retirement, an **in-house inquiry** was confirmed. **Probably the first recorded instance of a judge in India facing a disciplinary trial for hate speech.**

**Wajahat Khan / Sharmishta Panoli — Justice Nagarathna's three observations ★:**

> *"There is no question of taking it down, because **once it is posted, it is out there**."*
>
> *"Instead of the State having to regulate, why can't the citizens have **restraints themselves**?… If they don't, then the State will step in – and **who wants the State to step in?**"*
>
> *"**Having an opinion is one thing, but to say that in a particular way is an abuse**… there should be **FRATERNITY** between the citizens – then all this hate will come down."*

**Three ideas:** posts are **permanent** · **self-restraint** is better than State regulation · **opinion is different from the manner of saying it**.

**Imran Pratapgarhi (poem case):** a poem that *"plainly translates to the suffering of injustice with love"* led to an FIR for **promoting enmity**. **Justice Oka:** *"Nobody has any respect for creativity… **Some sensibility has to be shown by the police. They should at least have read the poem and understood**… That is respect for free speech and expression."*

**Ali Khan Mahmudabad:** an **Ashoka University professor arrested for online posts on Operation Sindoor**. The Supreme Court **granted bail**, but the order included **conditions gagging him** from posts, articles or speech on the operation, and **tasked an SIT with interpreting the "hidden meaning"** behind his posts.

**"Dog whistling" ★** = communication that looks ordinary to the general audience but **carries a coded message** to a particular audience — saying one thing while meaning another.

## 7. *Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India* (SC, 2026) ★★★

*(Full case study in Part 5.)*

**The core question:** **can the Supreme Court create new criminal offences when Parliament has not?**

**The four holdings:**

1. **Courts neither create offences nor legislate punishments** — that is the legislature's duty. The Court **cannot issue mandamus directing Parliament to enact a law**.
2. The ***Vishaka*** power to issue interim guidelines applies **only where constitutional silence or legislative abeyance exists** — **not where the law already occupies the field**.
3. **The claim of a legislative vacuum on hate speech is "MISCONCEIVED".** *"The mere occurrence of incidents of hate speech cannot lead to the conclusion that the law is silent on the subject. More often than not, the difficulty lies in the **EFFECTIVE ENFORCEMENT AND APPLICATION** of the existing statutory framework."*
4. **No continuing mandamus** — it would amount to **"MICRO-MANAGEMENT"** of executive functions.

> **The distinction to write ★:** *"No law exists"* is different from *"Law exists but is poorly enforced."* The Court said India's problem is the **second**.

## 8. Fake news

### Types of Information Disorder ★★

```
      ┌──────────────────────┐   ┌──────────────────────┐
      │      FALSENESS       │   │   INTENT TO HARM     │
      │                      │   │                      │
      │  MIS-        ┌───────┼───┼───────┐         MAL- │
      │  INFORMATION │   DISINFORMATION  │  INFORMATION │
      │              │  false + intent   │              │
      │  wrong       │  fabricated media │  TRUE, but   │
      │  captions,   │  conspiracy       │  weaponised: │
      │  dates,      │  theories,        │  revenge porn│
      │  satire      │  rumours          │  changed     │
      │  taken       └───────┼───┼───────┘  context     │
      │  seriously           │   │                      │
      └──────────────────────┘   └──────────────────────┘

              — Claire Wardle, FirstDraft (2019)

   MISINFORMATION vs DISINFORMATION  =  INTENT
   MALINFORMATION                    =  TRUE information, misused
```


**Claire Wardle, *'Understanding Information Disorder'* (FirstDraft, 2019).** Two dimensions: **FALSENESS** and **INTENT TO HARM**.

| | **Misinformation** | **Disinformation** | **Malinformation** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **False?** | **Yes** | **Yes** | **No — it is TRUE** |
| **Intent to harm?** | **No** | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| **What it is** | **Unintentional mistakes** — wrong captions, dates, statistics, translations, or **satire taken seriously** | **Fabricated or deliberately manipulated content**; **conspiracy theories or rumours** | **Deliberate publication of private information** for personal rather than public interest, e.g. revenge porn; **deliberate change of context, date or time** of genuine content |

**Two key distinctions:** misinformation vs disinformation = **INTENT** · malinformation is **TRUE information weaponised**.

### Elections — the Voluntary Code of Ethics

Fake news **"assumed a pernicious character during the 2019 general elections"** and in the **Haryana and Maharashtra assembly elections**. In **March 2019**, the **Social Media Platforms and IAMAI** submitted the **"Voluntary Code of Ethics for the General Elections 2019"** to the **Election Commission of India**, extended in **September 2019** to all future elections.

**The five commitments ★:**

1. **Voluntary information, education and communication campaigns** to build awareness of electoral laws
2. A **high priority dedicated grievance redressal channel** for cases reported by the **ECI**
3. A **notification mechanism** for the ECI to report **potential violations of Section 126 of the R.P. Act, 1951**
4. **All political advertisements pre-certified** by the **Media Certification and Monitoring Committees**
5. **Transparency in paid political advertisements** using **pre-existing labels/disclosure technology**

### *Alak Alok Srivastava v. UOI* (W.P. 468/2020, 31 March 2020)

**Facts:** the **nationwide movement of migrant labourers due to panic created by fake news** multiplied the Government's difficulties during the lockdown. **Some lost their lives.**

**The WHO quote the Court used:** *"We are not just fighting an epidemic; we are fighting an **infodemic**. Fake news spreads faster and more easily than this virus, and is just as dangerous."*

**Three directions:**

1. Media must **verify news before dissemination** and **publish the official version** of corona-related developments
2. **Section 54 of the Disaster Management Act 2005** invoked to **punish those who make or circulate such information**
3. The Government to start an **official portal for daily updates**

## 9. *Kunal Kamra v. UOI* (Bombay HC, 2024) ★★★

> **The slides are headed "Supreme Court on Fake News" but the case line says Bom. HC. It is a BOMBAY HIGH COURT decision.**

**What was challenged:** the **IT Amendment Rules 2023** inserted **Rule 3(1)(b)(v)**, empowering the Government to set up a **Fact-Checking Unit (FCU)** to **verify online content relating to the "business of the Government of India"** and label it **"false," "fake," or "misleading."**

**Grounds:**

| Ground | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Article 14** | Arbitrary — **applies only to digital media, not print media** |
| **Article 19(1)(a)** | Restricts speech about the Government |
| **Article 19(1)(g)** | Affects the profession and business of digital publishers |
| ***Ultra vires* s.79 and s.87** | The Rule exceeded the **rule-making power** and used **safe harbour** to control speech |
| **Censorship** | The Government becomes the **sole arbiter of truth about its own affairs** |
| **Vagueness** | **"Fake," "false," "misleading"** are overbroad and **could be misused against dissent** |

### The split verdict — three judges ★★

```
       Rule 3(1)(b)(v) — the Govt FACT CHECK UNIT — challenged
                              │
          ┌───────────────────┴───────────────────┐
          ▼                                       ▼
   JUSTICE NEELA GOKHALE                  JUSTICE GAUTAM PATEL
   ✔ UPHELD the Rule                      ✘ STRUCK IT DOWN
   • FCU only IDENTIFIES content          • online = offline parity
   • courts decide finally                • must fit Art.19(2)
   • bias must be PROVED, not presumed    • "OUTSOURCED CENSORSHIP"
          │                                       │
          └───────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                          ▼   SPLIT → referred to a third judge
              JUSTICE A.S. CHANDURKAR  (tie-breaker)
                     AGREES WITH JUSTICE PATEL
                          │
                          ▼
             ★ RULE HELD UNCONSTITUTIONAL
               • FALSITY is not an Art.19(2) ground
               • Art.14 — arbiter of truth in its own cause
               • UNGUIDED DISCRETION → self-censorship
```


**Justice Neela Gokhale — UPHELD the Rule:**

- Intermediaries lose safe harbour **only if they ignore procedures consistent with Article 19(2)** (relying on *Shreya Singhal* and *P. Krishnamurthy*)
- The FCU **only identifies** content; it is **not the final authority**. **Courts retain the ultimate power** to decide validity, so **due process was not compromised**
- **Bias must be specifically demonstrated, not presumed** merely from Government appointment

**Justice Gautam Patel — STRUCK DOWN the Rule:**

- **Article 19(1)(a) protects speech online just as offline**; restrictions must **strictly fall within Article 19(2)** and satisfy **proportionality**
- The Rule **forced intermediaries to censor content about the Government**, effectively **OUTSOURCING CENSORSHIP** and **stripping citizens of space for dissent or satire**

**Justice Chandurkar — the TIE-BREAKING opinion — AGREED WITH JUSTICE PATEL. The Rule was held UNCONSTITUTIONAL.**

His reasons:

1. Relied on ***Shreya Singhal*** and ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP*** — *"Restrictions placed must be justifiable under a law competent under clause (2) of Article 19"*
2. **Censoring information merely because it was "false" or "misleading" was IMPERMISSIBLE**
3. Under **Article 14**, the Rule was **discriminatory** because it let the Government be **arbiter of truth in its own cause**
4. The undefined terms gave the FCU **UNGUIDED DISCRETION**, creating a **genuine risk of self-censorship** — relying also on ***Anuradha Bhasin***

### The single most important point ★★★

> **Article 19(2) has NO ground called "falsity."** The Government cannot restrict speech merely by calling it false or misleading. It must fit one of the **eight listed grounds**.

> **s.70(1):** The appropriate Government may, **by notification in the Official Gazette**, declare any **computer resource which directly or indirectly affects the facility of Critical Information Infrastructure, to be a PROTECTED SYSTEM**.
>
> **Explanation:** **"Critical Information Infrastructure" means the computer resource, the incapacitation or destruction of which shall have a DEBILITATING IMPACT on NATIONAL SECURITY, ECONOMY, PUBLIC HEALTH OR SAFETY.**
>
> **s.70(2):** The Government may **authorise the persons who may access** protected systems.
>
> **s.70(3):** Whoever **secures access or ATTEMPTS to secure access** in contravention of this section shall be punished with imprisonment **up to TEN YEARS** and fine.
>
> **s.70(4):** The Central Government shall **prescribe the information security practices and procedures** for such protected system.

**The test is CONSEQUENCE, not size.** "Debilitating" means **weakening**.

**Why CII matters:** CIIs are the **backbone** of sectors like **transport, power, energy, telecom, PSUs, banking and finance**. Because of **cross-sectoral interconnectedness**, a breakdown of one CII has **"ripple effects"** on others, creating **cascading disasters**.

### The UIDAI notification — the worked example

> **G.S.R. 993(E), 11 December 2015** — under **s.70(1)**, the Central Government declares **UIDAI's Central Identities Data Repository (CIDR) facilities, Information Assets, Logistics Infrastructure and Dependencies** to be a **Protected System**.
>
> **Authorised personnel under s.70(2):** (1) designated UIDAI officers and support staff; (2) team members of the contracted **Managed Service Provider**; (3) other authorised third-party vendors and partners; (4) UIDAI authorised business partners.

**What it shows:** a notification **names the system** and **limits who may lawfully access it**. After that, unauthorised access carries **up to 10 years**.

# MODULE 5 — SYNTHETICALLY GENERATED INFORMATION (2026 RULES)

## 1. Why the Rules were made

1. **Rapid rise of deepfakes, misinformation and synthetically generated media in India**
2. **Generative AI has made synthetic content creation cheap and accessible**
3. **GenAI can generate highly realistic manipulated audio, video and text with minimal user input**
4. The **distinction between authentic and AI-generated content is increasingly difficult to identify**
5. **Traditional legal frameworks based on HUMAN AUTHORSHIP and TRACEABILITY are becoming inadequate**
6. Risks: **fraud, impersonation, misinformation, electoral manipulation, erosion of public trust**
7. Existing law under the **IT Act 2000** and **BNS 2023** addresses these harms only in a **fragmented and reactive** manner

**The thesis ★:**

> **The Rules SHIFT GOVERNANCE from REACTIVE LIABILITY-BASED regulation to TECHNOLOGY-EMBEDDED regulation.**

**In plain words:** earlier the law waited for harm and then imposed liability. Now the duty is built **into the platform's technology** — declarations, labels, metadata and detection.

## 2. The definitions ★★

### ★★ Is this content SGI? — the four-step test


### Rule 2(1)(ca) — "Audio, Visual or Audio-visual information"

> **"Any audio, image, photograph, graphic, video, moving visual recording, sound recording or any other audio, visual or audio-visual content, with or without accompanying audio, whether created, generated, modified or altered through any computer resource."**

### Rule 2(1)(wa) — "Synthetically Generated Information" ★★★

> **"audio, visual or audio-visual information which is ARTIFICIALLY OR ALGORITHMICALLY CREATED, GENERATED, MODIFIED OR ALTERED USING A COMPUTER RESOURCE, in a manner that such information APPEARS TO BE REAL, AUTHENTIC OR TRUE and DEPICTS OR PORTRAYS ANY INDIVIDUAL OR EVENT in a manner that IS, OR IS LIKELY TO BE PERCEIVED AS, INDISTINGUISHABLE FROM A NATURAL PERSON OR REAL-WORLD EVENT."**

**Three cumulative elements:**

| Part | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **1. Media** | Audio, visual or audio-visual information |
| **2. Technical** | **Artificially or algorithmically** created, generated, modified or altered **using a computer resource** |
| **3. Perception** | It **appears real, authentic or true** and depicts a person or event in a way that **is, or is likely to be perceived as, indistinguishable** from reality |

### The Proviso — what is NOT SGI

| Excluded | Condition |
|---|---|
| **Routine or good-faith editing/enhancement** | **without misrepresentation** |
| **Routine, good-faith creation of documents/materials** | **without false documents or records** |
| **Tools used only for accessibility, clarity, translation or searchability** | **without manipulating a material part** |

**In plain words:** colour-correcting a photo is fine. Swapping someone's face into it is not.

## 3. SGI as "information" and the safe-harbour fix ★

**Rules 2(1A) and 2(1B)** bring SGI within the IT Rules 2021 and the **s.79 safe harbour framework**. Intermediaries may use **reasonable technical measures, including automated tools, to detect, label, remove or disable access to SGI**.

**No automatic loss of safe harbour:** such measures, when compliant with the 2026 Rules, **will NOT by themselves amount to "selecting or modifying" content under s.79(2)(a)–(b)**.

**Why this was necessary ★:** **s.79(2)(b)** says the intermediary must **not select or modify** information. But the 2026 Rules **require** detection and labelling. **Without this clarification, obeying the new Rules would destroy the safe harbour.**

## 4. New obligations on intermediaries

| Rule | Obligation |
|---|---|
| **3(1)(c)** | Inform users **every 3 months**, in a **simple, clear and effective** manner, of: **consequences of violating platform rules · suspension or termination of access · exposure to civil and criminal liability · mandatory reporting obligations** |
| **3(1)(ca)** | Platforms enabling SGI must **clearly inform users of the legal risks**. Enforcement actions: **immediate removal**; **suspension or permanent termination with EVIDENCE PRESERVATION**; **disclosure of identity to the victim "as per law"**; **mandatory reporting "where legally required"** |
| **3(1)(cb)** | On **awareness** of unlawful SGI, take **prompt and appropriate actions** |
| **3(3)(a)** ★ | **Mandatory labelling of LAWFUL SGI** — **clear and prominent identification** as SGI, plus **PERMANENT METADATA / technical provenance including UNIQUE IDENTIFIERS for traceability and authentication** |
| **3(3)(b)** | **Prevent modification, suppression or removal** of labels, metadata and unique identifiers |

> **Key point: lawful SGI is NOT banned. It must be LABELLED.** Only **unlawful** SGI must be removed.

> **"As per law" and "where legally required" matter** — they mean an **independent legal basis** is needed before identity is disclosed.

## 7. Constitutional problems — the three issues ★★★

*(The most likely essay question in this module.)*

### Issue 1 — Pre-publication screening = PRIOR RESTRAINT

- The scheme requires **declaration + verification BEFORE upload**
- **Indian jurisprudence:** ***Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi*** and ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras*** **opposed pre-censorship and favoured post-publication accountability**
- **Intermediaries become gatekeepers** — a shift from **COURTS → PRIVATE PLATFORMS**
- **Collateral censorship risk:** **platforms over-censor to avoid liability**
- **Proportionality FAILS** on **least restrictive means** and at the **balancing stage**
- **Result: DE FACTO PRIOR RESTRAINT + PRIVATISED CENSORSHIP**

### Issue 2 — Overbreadth and vagueness

- **Overbreadth** = the rule **regulates excessive speech**. **Vagueness** = **unclear standards → arbitrary enforcement**
- **"Appears real"** and **"likely to be perceived"** are **subjective**
- **Parallel with *Shreya Singhal*: vague terms → unconstitutional**
- **Automated tools** produce **inconsistent, error-prone** classification
- **Outcome: over-removal of lawful content → strong chilling effect**

### Issue 3 — Privacy

- **Right to privacy under Article 21** — ***K.S. Puttaswamy***
- **Legitimate aim:** protection of victims of **deepfakes, impersonation and NCII**
- **Concern: identity disclosure WITHOUT judicial oversight**
- **Risks:** misuse, harassment, retaliation, suppression of legitimate speech
- **Proportionality issue: lacks procedural safeguards and independent review**
- **Implication: threat to informational privacy and due process**

## 8. Operational and technical problems

| Issue | Content |
|---|---|
| **Arms race** | **Detection improves → generation tools adapt and evade** (GANs) |
| **False positives** | **Lawful content flagged** — satire, parody, journalism |
| **False negatives** | **Harmful deepfakes go undetected** |
| **Regulatory gap** | No clear rules on **handling errors, user notification, or appeal** |
| **Metadata limits** | Watermarks **degrade** through **compression, editing, reformatting** |
| **Scale and context** | Classification needs **intent analysis** and distinguishing **harm from satire**; automated tools are often insufficient |
| **Indian context** | **Multilingual and culturally diverse** content; risk of **misinterpretation** |
| **Outcome** | **Risk-averse moderation → over-removal of lawful speech** |

> **Note the contradiction ★:** Rule 3(3)(a) demands **PERMANENT** metadata, but metadata **degrades** through ordinary compression and editing.

## 9. The way forward

1. **Technology-driven compliance** — advanced AI detection tools, metadata and content-authenticity standards
2. **Increasing role of courts** — clarify the boundary between **creative expression and harmful manipulation**; **protect satire, parody and artistic freedom**
3. **Global cooperation** — AI content crosses borders; need **traceability and authenticity standards** and **regulatory consistency**
4. **Public awareness and digital literacy** — **regulation alone cannot tackle misinformation**
5. **Towards a comprehensive digital law** — the current amendments may be an **interim measure**, with an expected transition to a broader **Digital India Act**

# PART 5 — THE TWO CASE STUDIES

## CASE STUDY 1 — *Neetu Singh v. Telegram FZ LLC* (2022) ★★★

| | |
|---|---|
| **Case number** | **CS (COMM) 282/2020** |
| **Bench** | **Justice Pratibha M. Singh, Delhi High Court** |
| **Petitioners** | **Ms. Neetu Singh** and **K.D. Campus Pvt. Ltd.** |
| **Respondent** | **Telegram FZ LLC** and **unknown persons** |

**Relevant law:** *Puttaswamy* (2017) 10 SCC 1 · *MySpace* (2016) · **IT Act ss.69, 72A, 79, 81** · **IT Rules 2021** · **Copyright Act ss.2(f), 2(o), 14, 63** · **Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act 2012**.

### Background

1. The plaintiffs' **video lectures, books and educational materials** were **shared without authorisation on Telegram**.
2. On request, **some channels were taken down, but new infringing channels were repeatedly created** — often with **minor modifications such as prefixes or suffixes** to the name.
3. They sued for a **permanent injunction, damages and costs**, and sought **disclosure of the identity of the persons operating the channels**.
4. **Telegram refused**, relying on: **privacy** (*Puttaswamy*), **freedom of expression**, its **privacy policy**, the **IT Rules 2021**, and the fact that its **servers with encrypted data are in Singapore** under the **PDPA 2012**.

### The core legal question

> **"Whether Telegram can be directed to disclose the identity of the creators of the infringing channels which unauthorizedly and illegally disseminate the Plaintiffs' copyrighted works?"**

### The Court's analysis — four points ★★

**1. Jurisdiction despite foreign servers.** Since the **infringement was concentrated in Delhi** and the content was **specifically relevant to India** (material for **Indian competitive examinations**), jurisdiction **could not be excluded merely because the servers were abroad**.

> *"Courts in India would be perfectly justified in directing Telegram, which runs its massive operations in India, to adhere to Indian law… **Infringers cannot be permitted to seek shelter under Telegram's policies merely on the ground that its physical server is in Singapore.**"*

**2. Copyright.** The course materials are **"literary works" (s.2(o))**; the compiled lectures are **"cinematograph films" (s.2(f))**. **Section 14** gives the owner **exclusive rights including electronic reproduction and distribution**. So the dissemination was **infringement**.

**3. Intermediary liability.** Under **s.79(3)(b)**, Telegram had to **remove or disable access while preserving evidence**. **Rule 3** prohibits intermediaries from hosting content that **infringes copyright**. **So Telegram could not rely on intermediary protections to avoid its obligations.**

**4. Privacy rejected.**

> *"The right to freedom of speech or the right to life including the right to privacy **cannot be used by any person or entity, let alone an infringer, in order to escape the consequences of illegal actions.**"*

> *"From the ratio of **K.S. Puttaswamy** it is clear… **if there is a law in existence to justify the disclosure** and **there is a need for the disclosure** considering the nature of encroachment of the right, then **privacy cannot be a ground to justify non-disclosure, so long as the same is not disproportionate.**"*

**The three-part test ★:** (1) **a law must exist** authorising disclosure; (2) **disclosure must be needed**; (3) **it must not be disproportionate**.

### The decision

1. Servers in Singapore **could not prevent disclosure** of information about infringement occurring in India — otherwise **copyright holders would be left without an effective remedy**.
2. **Telegram was directed to disclose** details of the infringing channels and devices.
3. **The details — mobile numbers, IP addresses, email addresses etc. — were to be given to the Court in a SEALED COVER.**

**The "sealed cover" point matters:** the information went **confidentially to the Court**, not on the public record. That keeps the disclosure **proportionate**.

## CASE STUDY 2 — *Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India* ★★★

| | |
|---|---|
| **Case number** | **Writ Petition [C] No. 943 of 2021** |
| **Bench** | **Justice Vikram Nath and Justice Sandeep Mehta** |

**Relevant law:** **Articles 14, 19, 21** · **Indian Penal Code** · *Kesavananda Bharati* · ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*** (1997) 6 SCC 241 · *Tehseen Poonawalla* (2018) · *Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan* (2014) · *Lalita Kumari* · *Union of India v. S.B. Vohra*.

### Background

A **batch of writ petitions, appeals and contempt petitions filed between 2020 and 2025** concerning **hate speech, communal incitement and fake or inflammatory news**. A major background event was the **COVID-19 pandemic and the Tablighi Jamaat / Nizamuddin Markaz incident**.

**The petitioners came from both sides** — some alleging hate speech against **Muslims** (Peace Party of India, Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, Qurban Ali, Shaheen Abdulla, Firoz Iqbal Khan on the *"Bindas Bol"* programme), one alleging hate speech against **Hindus** (Cholayil Sashidharan), and Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay seeking **examination of the legal framework on hate speech and rumour-mongering**.

**Since the petitions raised substantially similar questions, the Supreme Court CLUBBED them.**

### The core legal question

> **"Can the Supreme Court create new criminal offences or formulate new criminal law through judicial directions when Parliament has not specifically enacted such offences?"**

### The four issues ★★

| Issue | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Can the Court **create or expand criminal offences**? | **NO** |
| **II** | Is the **substantive criminal law sufficient**, or is the field **unoccupied**? | **Sufficient — the field is OCCUPIED** |
| **III** | Does **procedural law offer adequate remedies** on **non-registration of FIRs**? | **YES** |
| **IV** | Should a **"continuing mandamus"** be issued? | **NO** |

### Issue I — Separation of powers

The Court quoted **Montesquieu's *The Spirit of Laws***:

> *"While exercise of powers by the legislature and executive is subject to judicial restraint, **the only check on our own exercise of power is the self-imposed discipline of JUDICIAL RESTRAINT**."*

> *"**The courts neither create offences nor do they introduce or legislate punishments. It is the duty of the legislature.**"*

***Vishaka* distinguished:** in that case the Court relied on an **International Convention — CEDAW**. Here, *"the legislature has enacted the law and provided the punishment… **There is no constitutional silence or abeyance.**"* And *"**This Court cannot provide a higher punishment. It can only suggest to the legislature.**"*

| Courts CAN | Courts CANNOT |
|---|---|
| Fill gaps | **Entrench upon the field of legislation** |
| Issue binding **directions for enforcing the laws** | **Create offences** |
| **Lay down time-limits** for proceedings | **Introduce or legislate punishments** |
| Redeem injustice in the case before them | **Provide a higher punishment** |

### Issue II — No legislative vacuum ★★★

> **"The submission that there exists a legislative or constitutional vacuum in addressing hate speech and allied offences is MISCONCEIVED."**

> **"The mere occurrence of incidents of hate speech cannot lead to the conclusion that the law is silent on the subject. More often than not, the difficulty lies in the EFFECTIVE ENFORCEMENT AND APPLICATION of the existing statutory framework."**

**The Court referred to the Law Commission's 267th Report (2017) on Hate Speech** by **Dr. Justice (Retd.) B.S. Chauhan**, covering the IPC, the RP Act 1951, the Protection of Civil Rights Act, the Religious Institutions (Prevention of Misuse) Act, the Cable Television Network Regulation Act, the Cinematograph Act and the CrPC — and specifically **ss.153A, 153B, 295A, 298 and 505 IPC (now the BNS)**.

> **The distinction to write:** **"No law exists"** vs **"Law exists but is poorly enforced."**

> *"Once the Court arrives at the conclusion that **no legislative vacuum exists**, the principle of **judicial restraint** assumes considerable significance."*

### Issue III — Procedural remedies are adequate

Relying on ***Lalita Kumari v. Government of UP***: the statutory scheme of the CrPC **"leaves no discretion with the police in such cases and MANDATES REGISTRATION OF AN FIR."**

And **s.156(3) CrPC**: where the police have not investigated properly, the **Magistrate can direct and monitor the investigation** (though he cannot investigate himself).

> **"The function of this Court is NOT TO CREATE NEW OFFENCES OR CONSTRUCT PARALLEL REGULATORY REGIMES, but to ensure FAITHFUL IMPLEMENTATION OF THE REMEDIES ALREADY ENVISAGED UNDER LAW."**

### Issue IV — No continuing mandamus

Citing ***Union of India v. S.B. Vohra***, **mandamus lies where a LEGAL RIGHT is established and a corresponding PUBLIC DUTY remains UNPERFORMED**, and **no equally efficacious remedy exists**.

Here the law **already incorporates adequate checks and balances**, and intervention is warranted **only upon a demonstrated failure**. A continuing mandamus would amount to **"MICRO-MANAGEMENT"** of executive functions and would be **neither feasible nor consistent with the constitutional scheme**. The Court also cited ***National Federation of Indian Women*** — national-level continuous monitoring across States would be **impractical and constitutionally inappropriate**.

### The fraternity passage ★

> **"Hate speech is thus not merely a deviation from acceptable discourse; it is fundamentally ANTITHETICAL TO THE CONSTITUTIONAL VALUE OF FRATERNITY and strikes at the moral fabric of our Republic… The land historically known as Bharata has, across centuries, been a refuge for diverse communities fleeing persecution, offering not merely shelter but acceptance and assimilation."**

### How to characterise the ruling ★

> The ruling is **a judgment of INSTITUTIONAL RESTRAINT rather than expansion of rights**. The Court **neither widened nor restricted** free expression; it **firmly declined to judicially legislate** where **Parliament had already acted but enforcement had been inconsistent**.

## C. TWENTY LIKELY PROBLEM QUESTIONS WITH ANSWER SKELETONS

### Q1 [10 marks] — E-commerce safe harbour

> *ShopLux is an online marketplace with 80 lakh registered users in India. It onboards sellers, photographs and lists their products, stores them in its warehouse, packs them in ShopLux-branded boxes, guarantees authenticity, uses brand names in its meta-tags, and handles payment and delivery. A luxury brand finds counterfeits on ShopLux and emails: "counterfeits of our products are being sold on your platform." ShopLux does nothing. Advise the brand.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Classify:** intermediary under **s.2(1)(w)** ("online market place" is in the list). **80 lakh > 50 lakh** → also an **SSMI** (S.O. 942(E)), so **Rule 4** applies.
- **s.79(2):** its function is **not limited** to providing access (clause (a)), and it is **not passive** (clause (b)) — it **selects** sellers and **modifies** listings.
- ***Christian Louboutin* (2018):** the **26 activities** test. ShopLux performs at least eight — onboarding, listing, warehousing, packing in branded boxes, authenticity guarantees, meta-tags, payment, delivery. Para 78: **"active participation in the selling process"** deprives it of the exemption. **Safe harbour lost.**
- **But the notice is defective.** ***MySpace* (DB, 2016):** liability needs **actual knowledge, not general awareness**. Para 57 — the owner must give **specific information including URLs**, and **the burden is on the rightsholder**. ***Kent RO*** — **no duty to pre-screen**; that would make the platform an **adjudicator**.
- **Advice:** serve a **URL-specific notice**. If ShopLux then fails to act, **s.79(3)(b)** is attracted, **Rule 3(1)(b)(iv)** is breached, and **Rule 7** removes s.79(1) entirely. Removed material must be preserved **180 days** under **Rule 3(1)(g)**.
- **Note:** s.79 is lost on **active participation** alone, independent of the notice point. **Trade Marks Act 1999 applies alongside** — the proviso to **s.81** preserves it.

### Q2 [10 marks] — Actual knowledge and a private complaint

> *A private individual emails a social media platform alleging that a post about him is defamatory, and demands removal within 24 hours. The platform does not remove it. Three months later he sues, arguing the platform lost safe harbour. Advise the platform.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Issue:** does a **private complaint** constitute "actual knowledge" under **s.79(3)(b)**?
- **s.79(3)(b)** needs **two elements**: actual knowledge **AND** failure to remove expeditiously.
- ***Shreya Singhal* (2015), para 119(c):** s.79(3)(b) is **READ DOWN**. Actual knowledge arises **only** from **(a) a court order**, or **(b) notification by the appropriate Government or its agency**, and the unlawful act must be **relatable to Article 19(2)**.
- **Why:** a private complaint would make the platform remove first and judge later — **the Court put the legality decision back with courts and authorised agencies**.
- **Apply:** the email is neither. **No actual knowledge arose. Safe harbour intact** — provided the platform satisfies **s.79(2)(a) or (b)** and **(c) due diligence**.
- **Caution:** the individual should obtain a **court order**; the platform must then act within **3 hours** under **Rule 3(1)(d)** as amended in 2026.

### Q3 [10 marks] — Extra-territorial jurisdiction

> *A person in Country X, who has never visited India and is not an Indian national, hacks into the servers of an Indian bank located in Mumbai and steals customer data. He argues Indian law does not apply to him. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **s.1(2):** the Act extends to the whole of India **and applies to any offence or contravention committed outside India by any person**. The IT Act is a **long-arm statute**.
- **s.75(1):** applies **irrespective of nationality**.
- **s.75(2):** the nexus requirement — it applies **if the act involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India**. Here the **servers are in Mumbai**. **Nexus satisfied.**
- **Offences:** **s.43** (contravention — unauthorised access, downloading data; **compensation**) and **s.66** (same acts done **dishonestly or fraudulently** — an **offence**). If the bank's system is a **notified protected system**, **s.70(3)** applies — **up to 10 years**.
- **Conclusion:** his nationality and location are **irrelevant**. The **location of the computer** decides. Enforcement may require extradition or mutual legal assistance, but **jurisdiction exists**.

### Q4 [5 marks] — The First Schedule

> *A dying man executes his will as a PDF, signs it with a digital signature, and emails it to his son. Is it valid under the IT Act?*

**Skeleton:**

- **s.1(4):** *"Nothing in this Act shall apply to documents or transactions specified in the First Schedule."*
- **Entry 4** of the First Schedule: **a will as defined in s.2(h), Indian Succession Act 1925, including any other testamentary disposition**.
- **So the IT Act does not apply.** The electronic will gets **no legal recognition** under ss.3 to 10. It must satisfy the Succession Act, which requires **attestation by two witnesses**.
- **Why excluded:** high formality requirement and **high risk of fraud**.
- **Note:** under the **proviso to s.1(4)**, the Central Government may amend the Schedule by notification laid before Parliament — so this could change.

### Q5 [5 marks] — Is a smart device a "computer"?

> *B enters A's house without permission and operates A's smart washing machine, which uses sensors and an on-board receiver that processes readings and adjusts water and electricity. A files a case for unauthorised access to a computer. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Issue:** does the appliance fall within **s.2(1)(i)**?
- **s.2(1)(i)** requires a device or system performing **logical, arithmetic and memory functions** by manipulating impulses, **including connected input, output, processing and storage facilities**.
- **Apply:** fuzzy logic = **logical**; calculating water/electricity = **arithmetic**; storing readings = **memory**; sensors = **input**; controls = **output**; the receiver = **processing**. **It qualifies**, at minimum as a **computer system** under **s.2(1)(l)**.
- ***Diebold*** **distinguished**: an ATM was held not to be a computer because **processing happens on a remote server**. Here processing is **on-board**. So *Diebold* supports A.
- **Conclusion:** **s.43(a)** — access without permission — is attracted, giving **compensation**. **s.66** additionally requires **dishonest or fraudulent** intention, which is doubtful. A's stronger remedies are **trespass** and **s.43**.

### Q6 [10 marks] — Government fact-check unit

> *A State Government creates a "Verification Cell" and directs platforms to remove within three hours any post it labels "misleading" about the State's flood relief. A journalist's critical post is flagged and removed. Advise the journalist.*

**Skeleton:**

- **The right:** the post is protected by **Article 19(1)(a)**. Per ***Anuradha Bhasin***, speech **through the internet** is an integral part of Article 19(1)(a).
- **Article 19(2) has eight grounds** — sovereignty and integrity, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement. **"Misleading" is not one.**
- ***Kunal Kamra v. UOI* (Bombay HC, 2024):** **Justice Chandurkar's tie-breaking opinion** — *"censoring information merely because it was 'false' or 'misleading' was impermissible."* **Rule 3(1)(b)(v) held UNCONSTITUTIONAL.**
- **Article 14:** the Government acting as **arbiter of truth in its own cause** is discriminatory.
- **Vagueness:** "misleading" is **undefined and open-ended** → **unguided discretion** → **risk of self-censorship**. Apply the ***Shreya Singhal*** formula: *"There is no demarcating line conveyed by any of these expressions."*
- **Nature of the speech:** criticism of relief work is **discussion or advocacy**, not **incitement**. *"All other forms of speech, even if offensive or unpopular, have to be protected."*
- **Proportionality:** fails at **least restrictive means** — an **official portal and correction notices** would do, as directed in ***Alak Alok Srivastava***.
- **Conclusion:** the direction is liable to be struck down under **Articles 19(1)(a) and 14**; if it is a delegated instrument, also **ultra vires**. The journalist may also invoke **Rule 4(8)** — notice, reasons and an opportunity to dispute before removal.

### Q7 [10 marks] — Protected system and CII

> *A private company runs the software controlling a city's water supply. A contractor's employee whose access was revoked last month logs in with retained credentials and alters chemical dosing. The company has no CISO and no documented network architecture. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Is it CII?** Explanation to **s.70**: a computer resource whose **incapacitation or destruction would have a debilitating impact on national security, economy, public health or safety**. A city water supply satisfies **public health and safety**.
- **Four NCIIPC criteria:** size and economic value · criticality of the process · duration of non-availability · dependency of other sectors. All point to CII.
- **Has it been notified as a protected system under s.70(1)?** (Compare **G.S.R. 993(E), 11 December 2015** for UIDAI's CIDR.)
  - **If notified:** **s.70(3)** — securing access **or attempting to** in contravention → **up to 10 years and fine**. Under **s.70(2)** the notification lists authorised persons; the ex-contractor is not one.
  - **If not notified:** **s.43** (contravention, compensation) and **s.66** (dishonest/fraudulent — offence). **s.66F (cyber terrorism)** is arguable given the threat to public health.
- **The company's own liability:** if it is a protected system, the **2018 Rules** are triggered — it must have an **ISSC, CISO, ISMS, documented network architecture, Cyber Crisis Management Plan, inventory, SLAs and VTR assessment**. **No CISO and no documentation = breach.**
- **Institutions:** **NCIIPC (s.70A)** for a critical sector; **Rule 4(5)** — the **basic responsibility lies with the agency running the CII**, i.e. the company. **CERT-In (s.70B)** for incident response. **CERT-In Directions 2022** require **180 days of logs within Indian jurisdiction**.
- **Conclusion:** the employee is liable under **s.70(3)** or **ss.43/66**; the company is independently exposed for **2018 Rules non-compliance** and **log failures**. The root cause is **access control** — the **"PROCESS" limb of the 3 P's**.

### Q8 [5 marks] — Director liability

> *Police prosecute the Managing Director of an online marketplace under s.67 IT Act for an obscene video listed by a user. The company itself has not been made an accused. Advise the MD.*

**Skeleton:**

- **s.85:** where the person committing the contravention **is a company**, **every person in charge of and responsible to the company, as well as the company**, is guilty.
- ***Avnish Bajaj v. State*** **(Bazee.com, SC 2012):** **vicarious liability cannot be fastened** on the director where the company is not arraigned. **"Commission of an offence by the company was an express CONDITION PRECEDENT to attract the liability of others in charge of the company."**
- **Apply:** the company has not been made an accused. **The condition precedent fails.** Proceedings against the MD are liable to be **quashed**.
- **Add:** the **proviso to s.85** also protects a person who proves the contravention took place **without his knowledge** or that he exercised **all due diligence**.

### Q9 [5 marks] — Electronic obscenity: IT Act or IPC?

> *A manager of a website is discharged of the charge under s.67 IT Act for hosting obscene content. The prosecution then frames a charge under s.292 IPC on the same facts. Is this permissible?*

**Skeleton:**

- **No.** ***Sharat Babu Digumarti v. Govt of NCT of Delhi* (2016).**
- **HELD:** electronic forms of transmission are covered by the **IT Act, which is a SPECIAL LAW**. If the offender has been **acquitted or discharged under the IT Act provisions**, he **cannot be proceeded against under the IPC for the same offence**.
- **Principle:** a **special law prevails over a general law**. **s.81** also gives the IT Act **overriding effect**.
- **Conclusion:** the s.292 IPC charge must be **quashed**.

### Q10 [10 marks] — Disclosure of user identity, foreign servers

> *A publisher finds its e-books circulating on channels of a messaging app. Channels are taken down but new ones appear with slight name changes. The app refuses to disclose operator details, citing user privacy and the fact that its encrypted servers are in Country Y under that country's data protection law. Advise the publisher.*

**Skeleton:**

- ***Neetu Singh v. Telegram* (Delhi HC, 2022)** is directly on point.
- **Copyright:** the e-books are **"literary works" under s.2(o)**; **s.14** gives exclusive rights including **electronic reproduction and distribution**. The copies are **infringing copies**.
- **Jurisdiction:** *"Infringers cannot be permitted to seek shelter under Telegram's policies merely on the ground that its physical server is in Singapore."* Where infringement is **concentrated in India** and the content is **specifically relevant to India**, jurisdiction is **not excluded by foreign server location**.
- **Intermediary duty:** under **s.79(3)(b)**, the app must **remove or disable access while preserving evidence**; **Rule 3** prohibits hosting copyright-infringing content. It **cannot rely on intermediary protection** to avoid these duties.
- **Privacy:** *"The right to… privacy cannot be used by any person or entity, let alone an infringer, in order to escape the consequences of illegal actions."* Applying ***Puttaswamy***: disclosure is permissible if **(1) a law exists**, **(2) disclosure is needed**, and **(3) it is not disproportionate**.
- **Relief:** an order to disclose **mobile numbers, IP addresses and email addresses** of the channels/devices **to the Court in a SEALED COVER** — which is what keeps it **proportionate**.

### Q11 [5 marks] — First originator

> *A false message about a community goes viral on a messaging service and leads to a mob lynching. Investigating officers ask the service to identify who first sent it. The service says this requires breaking end-to-end encryption. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Rule 4(2), IT Rules 2021** — an SSMI **providing services primarily in the nature of messaging** must **enable identification of the FIRST ORIGINATOR**.
- **Triggered only by:** a **judicial order of a competent court**, **or** an order under **s.69** through the **2009 Interception Rules**. A police request alone is **not enough**.
- **The four provisos:**
  1. Only for offences relating to **sovereignty and integrity, security of the State, friendly relations, public order**, incitement, or **rape / sexually explicit material / CSAM**, punishable with **imprisonment of not less than FIVE YEARS**
  2. **No order** if **less intrusive means** would work
  3. The **contents** of the message need **not** be disclosed
  4. If the first originator is **outside India**, the **first originator within India is deemed** to be the first originator
- **Apply:** an offence leading to lynching engages **public order** and is punishable above five years, so proviso 1 is satisfied — **but a court or s.69 order is essential**, and the investigators must show **no less intrusive means** exist.

### Q12 [5 marks] — Blocking without a hearing

> *A platform receives a takedown notice from a government department under s.79(3)(b), with no reasons and no hearing. It argues that only s.69A permits blocking, and that notice is invalid. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **The distinction:** **s.69A** is a **Government POWER to block**, exercised through the **Blocking Rules 2009**, which provide a **hearing and written reasons**. **s.79(3)(b)** is a **CONDITION on the intermediary's immunity** — it **confers no independent blocking power**.
- ***X v. Union of India* (Karnataka HC, 2025):** the Court **rejected** the challenge. The **Sahyog Portal is within the four corners of the law**, and under **s.79(3)(b)**, if the Government or its agency sends a **lawful notification**, the intermediary **must take the content down or forfeit immunity under s.79**.
- **Conclusion:** the platform **cannot ignore the notice**. Non-compliance means loss of safe harbour, and via **Rule 7** liability under any law in force. But the **notice must be lawful** — the platform can challenge it if the issuing authority is not a competent, authorised agency.

### Q13 [10 marks] — Platform-wide blocking and proportionality

> *Some channels on a messaging app are used to leak examination papers. The Government blocks the entire app nationwide. The app has 12 crore lawful Indian users and had already removed most flagged URLs. Advise the app.*

**Skeleton:**

- **The power:** **s.69A** + **Blocking Rules 2009**, on grounds including **public order** and **preventing incitement to a cognizable offence**.
- ***Telegram v. Union of India* (Delhi HC, 2026)** is directly comparable — there Telegram had removed **900 of 1300 flagged URLs**, used **AI and human moderators**, and argued a platform-wide ban was **not the least restrictive method** and affected **150 million lawful users**, and that s.69A allows blocking of **"information", not whole platforms**.
- **The Court upheld the block** because the Government had **recorded sufficient reasons**, especially in an **emergency context**; the **Blocking Rules procedure** (interim order, opportunity to be heard, review) was followed; and **proportionality was satisfied**.
- **Apply proportionality (Puttaswamy / Anuradha Bhasin):** (1) **legitimate aim** — integrity of public examinations, yes; (2) **backed by law** — s.69A, yes; (3) **rational nexus** — yes; (4) **least intrusive means** — this is the **strongest argument for the app**: URL-level blocking was already working, so a full block may be excessive.
- ***Anuradha Bhasin*:** orders must be **reasoned, published, limited in duration and periodically reviewed**.
- **Conclusion:** the app's best case is **step 4 and the duration point**. On the *Telegram* precedent, however, a **time-limited** block with **recorded reasons in an emergency** is likely to be upheld.

### Q14 [5 marks] — Pre-screening demand

> *A brand asks a marketplace to filter every new listing for trademark infringement before it goes live. The marketplace refuses. Who is right?*

**Skeleton:**

- **The marketplace.** ***Kent RO Systems v. Amit Kotak* (Delhi HC, 2017).**
- **HELD:** the Court **refused to compel intermediaries to screen IP-infringing content on an *ex ante* basis**. Doing so would be **"an unreasonable interference with the rights of the intermediary to carry on its business"**, and would change its role **from a FACILITATOR to an ADJUDICATOR**.
- ***MySpace* (DB, 2016)** also rejected **general monitoring** and required **specific, URL-identified notice**.
- **But:** once **specific infringing listings are brought to its attention**, the marketplace **must remove them**.
- **Exception worth noting:** ***Sabu Mathew George*** **did** require **auto-blocking** of pre-natal sex determination advertisements — but that enforces a **specific statutory ban (s.22 PCPNDT Act)**.

### Q15 [10 marks] — Deepfake video

> *An AI-generated video showing a well-known actor endorsing an investment scheme is uploaded to a platform with 2 crore Indian users. It is realistic enough that viewers believe it. Several people lose money. Advise on the platform's obligations.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Is it SGI?** **Rule 2(1)(wa)** — three elements: **(1) media** — a video; **(2) technical** — artificially or algorithmically generated using a computer resource; **(3) perception** — it **appears real** and depicts an individual in a way **likely to be perceived as indistinguishable** from a natural person. **All satisfied.** The proviso does not help — this is not good-faith editing.
- **Is it unlawful SGI?** Yes — **impersonation** and **fraud**. **Rule 3(1)(b)** prohibits content that **impersonates another person**.
- **SSMI status:** **2 crore > 50 lakh**, so **Rule 4 applies**, including **Rule 4(1A)**: **mandatory user declaration before publication**, **technical verification** of that declaration, **prominent labelling**, and **SGI cannot be published without both declaration and label**. The platform appears to have failed **pre-publication verification**.
- **Rule 3(3)(a) and (b):** lawful SGI must carry **clear labelling** and **permanent metadata with unique identifiers**, and the platform must **prevent their removal**.
- **On notice:** **Rule 3(1)(d)** — on a court order or Government notification, remove within **3 HOURS**. If a victim complains of **impersonation or morphed images**, **Rule 3(2)(b)** — **2 HOURS**.
- **Rule 3(1)(ca):** enforcement actions include **removal**, **account termination with evidence preservation**, **disclosure of identity to the victim "as per law"**, and **reporting to authorities "where legally required"**.
- **Consequence:** failure to observe the Rules → **Rule 7** → **loss of s.79(1)** and liability under any law in force.
- **Also note:** **Rules 2(1A)/(1B)** confirm that using automated tools to detect and label does **not** by itself amount to "selecting or modifying" under **s.79(2)(a)–(b)** — so compliance does not cost the platform its safe harbour.

### Q16 [10 marks] — Challenge to the SGI Rules

> *A satire website challenges the 2026 Amendment Rules, arguing the pre-publication verification requirement is unconstitutional. Advise.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Issue 1 — Prior restraint.** The scheme requires **declaration + verification BEFORE upload**. ***Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi*** and ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras*** **opposed pre-censorship and favoured post-publication accountability**. The effect is **DE FACTO PRIOR RESTRAINT + PRIVATISED CENSORSHIP**, shifting the decision from **COURTS → PRIVATE PLATFORMS**, with a **collateral censorship** risk as platforms over-censor to avoid liability. Proportionality **fails** at **least restrictive means** and **balancing**.
- **Issue 2 — Overbreadth and vagueness.** **"Appears real"** and **"likely to be perceived"** are **subjective**. Apply ***Shreya Singhal***: vague terms produce **arbitrary enforcement** and a **chilling effect**. Satire is the clearest casualty — **false positives** flag **satire, parody and journalism**, and there are **no clear rules on handling errors, notification or appeal**.
- **Issue 3 — Privacy.** **Article 21** and ***Puttaswamy***. **Identity disclosure without judicial oversight**, with **no procedural safeguards or independent review**, threatens **informational privacy and due process**.
- **The Government's answer:** a **legitimate aim** — protecting victims of **deepfakes, impersonation and NCII**; the harms are **real** (fraud, electoral manipulation, erosion of public trust); and **lawful SGI is not banned, only labelled**.
- **Support from *Kunal Kamra*:** **falsity is not an Article 19(2) ground**, and undefined terms give **unguided discretion**.
- **Conclusion:** the strongest grounds are **vagueness** and **prior restraint**; the weakest is a blanket attack on labelling, since **labelling alone is a lighter measure than prohibition**.

### Q17 [5 marks] — FIR over a social media post

> *X posts a strongly worded criticism of a political leader. It is offensive to many and goes viral. An FIR is registered for "causing annoyance and promoting disharmony". Advise X.*

**Skeleton:**

- **s.66A was STRUCK DOWN** in ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)** — so any charge under it is void. Note that **FIRs continued to be registered under s.66A even after it was struck down**, and the Supreme Court had to reiterate its invalidity.
- **The test:** the Court distinguished **discussion, advocacy and incitement**. *"a speech can only be limited on grounds of the exceptions mentioned in Article 19(2) when it reaches the THRESHOLD OF INCITEMENT. All other forms of speech, even if OFFENSIVE or UNPOPULAR, have to be protected under Article 19(1)(a)."*
- **Apply:** criticism of a leader is **advocacy**, not incitement. **Offensiveness is not a ground.**
- **Vagueness:** "annoyance" was one of the very words the Court held **unconstitutionally vague** — *"What may cause annoyance or inconvenience to one may not cause annoyance or inconvenience to another."*
- **Add:** ***Imran Pratapgarhi*** — the police must **read and understand the content** before registering an FIR; *"That is respect for free speech and expression."*

### Q18 [5 marks] — NCIIPC or CERT-In?

> *(a) A power grid control system is attacked. (b) A small e-commerce startup suffers a ransomware attack. Which body handles each, and under which provision?*

**Skeleton:**

- **(a) Power grid** — a **critical sector** (one of NCIIPC's six: Transport, Power & Energy, Telecom, Government, BFSI, Strategic & Public Enterprises). **NCIIPC** under **s.70A** and the **2013 NCIIPC Rules** — its function under **Rule 4(2)** is to protect CII from **cyber-terrorist attack and cyber-warfare**. **NCIIPC is a unit of the NTRO** and works **24×7**. **Rule 4(5)** — responsibility is **co-shared** with the agency running the CII.
- **(b) Startup** — **non-critical**. **CERT-In** under **s.70B**, whose constituency is the **"Indian cyber community" (Rule 7)**. Its functions under **s.70B(4)** include **emergency measures for handling cyber security incidents**, **coordination of response**, and **advisories**.
- **Add for marks:** their functions sometimes **overlap**; the critical/non-critical distinction aids understanding but is **not watertight**.

### Q19 [5 marks] — CERT-In Directions and a VPN provider

> *A VPN provider operating in India refuses to keep subscriber records, saying its entire product is built on not identifying users. Advise on its legal position.*

**Skeleton:**

- **Legal basis:** the **CERT-In Directions 2022**, issued under the **CERT-In Rules 2013** read with **s.70B**.
- **Paragraph (iv):** all service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporate and Government organisations must **maintain logs of all ICT systems for a rolling period of 180 DAYS**, **within the Indian jurisdiction**.
- **Paragraph (v):** **VPN, VPS, cloud and data centre providers** must register and maintain for **5 YEARS**: validated **names**, **period of hire**, **IPs allotted**, **email/IP/time stamp at registration**, **purpose of hire**, **validated address and contact numbers**, and **ownership pattern**.
- **Apply:** the obligation is **mandatory**; the business model is not a defence.
- **The tension to note ★:** a VPN exists to **conceal the IP address and keep the user unidentifiable**. The Directions require the provider to **collect and retain exactly the identifying information** for five years. The conflict is between **privacy** and **incident investigation and accountability**.

### Q20 [10 marks] — Hate speech: is there a legal vacuum?

> *An NGO petitions the Supreme Court seeking a new offence of "hate speech" with a minimum sentence, and continuing judicial supervision of all hate speech cases nationwide. Advise on the likely outcome.*

**Skeleton:**

- ***Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India* (SC, 2026)** decides exactly this.
- **Issue I — can the Court create offences? NO.** Relying on **Montesquieu's separation of powers**: *"the courts neither create offences nor do they introduce or legislate punishments. It is the duty of the legislature."* The Court **cannot issue mandamus to the legislature** to enact a law, and *"cannot provide a higher punishment. It can only suggest to the legislature."*
- ***Vishaka* distinguished:** that power applies **only where constitutional silence or legislative abeyance exists** — there the Court relied on **CEDAW**. Here *"the legislature has enacted the law and provided the punishment."*
- **Issue II — is there a vacuum? NO.** *"The submission that there exists a legislative or constitutional vacuum… is **MISCONCEIVED**."* *"The mere occurrence of incidents of hate speech cannot lead to the conclusion that the law is silent… the difficulty lies in the **EFFECTIVE ENFORCEMENT AND APPLICATION** of the existing statutory framework."* The Court surveyed **ss.153A, 153B, 295A, 298, 505 IPC (now BNS)**, the **RP Act 1951**, the **Protection of Civil Rights Act**, the **Religious Institutions (Prevention of Misuse) Act**, the **Cable Television Networks Act**, the **Cinematograph Act** and the **CrPC**, and the **Law Commission's 267th Report**.
- **Issue III — remedies exist.** ***Lalita Kumari*** — **registration of an FIR is mandatory**; and **s.156(3) CrPC** lets a **Magistrate direct and monitor** an investigation.
- **Issue IV — no continuing mandamus.** It would be **"MICRO-MANAGEMENT"** of executive functions and **impractical** across States (*National Federation of Indian Women*).
- **Contrast with the earlier position:** ***Pravasi Bhalai*** referred the matter to the **Law Commission**; ***CAHS v. Karnataka*** declined to define hate speech for want of legislation.
- **Conclusion:** the petition will **fail**. The Court will **condemn hate speech in strong terms** — *"antithetical to the constitutional value of FRATERNITY"* — but will decline to legislate. The ruling is one of **institutional restraint**.
